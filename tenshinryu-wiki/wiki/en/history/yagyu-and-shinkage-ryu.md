# Tenshinryu and the Yagyū Clan: Shinkage-ryū, Lineage and Crests

**Short answer.** Tenshinryu is a branch of Shinkage-ryū (新陰流). Its founder, Tokizawa Yahei (時沢弥平), studied Shinkage-ryū under Yagyū Sekishūsai and then served Sekishūsai's son Yagyū Munenori as a personal retainer. Munenori is, in essence, the true origin of the style. The Tenshinryu flag carries the Tokugawa Aoi no Gomon and the Yagyū-gasa, the Yagyū clan's crest, because they stand for the legacy of the Kōgan, the covert Tokugawa corps for which Tenshinryu was created.

## Shinkage-ryū

Shinkage-ryū was founded by Kamiizumi Nobutsuna (上泉信綱) in the late Sengoku period, as a development of Kage-ryū (陰流). Yagyū Sekishūsai lost three matches to Kamiizumi in 1563, became his disciple, and in 1571 received the "Ikkoku Ichinin" license that made him Kamiizumi's successor. The style then became known as Yagyū-ryū and Yagyū Shinkage-ryū and passed down in the Yagyū family, which split into two lines:

- **Owari Yagyū**, led by Yagyū Toshitoshi (柳生利厳), instructor to the Owari Tokugawa family. It continues today in the Yagyū-kai and related groups.
- **Edo Yagyū**, led by Yagyū Munenori (柳生宗矩), sword instructor to the Tokugawa shoguns. After the Meiji Restoration the Edo Yagyū family gave up swordsmanship and its lineages ended. Tenshinryu is one of the few, and possibly the only, styles that traces its lineage to Edo Yagyū and Munenori.

Tenshinryu shares many technical elements with Shinkage-ryū, but it is not identical to it. Scrolls from Kamiizumi to Sekishūsai already show changes in technique names and procedures, and such adaptation was normal at the time.

## Tokizawa Yahei and Munenori

After the Yagyū fief was confiscated in 1585, the young Munenori wandered the provinces looking for a post. Tokizawa Yahei accompanied him. In 1594 Sekishūsai and Munenori met Tokugawa Ieyasu, who became Sekishūsai's disciple and took Munenori into his service. Munenori became sword instructor to Hidetada and Iemitsu, was made Sōmetsuke (Inspector General) in 1632, and became a daimyō in 1636, the only sword instructor ever to do so.

Under Munenori the Shirin-dan (士林団), a body of samurai working behind the scenes for the Tokugawa government, was formally named **Kōgan** (光願, "wishing for light"). Munenori then ordered Tokizawa to organize a complete martial system for the next generation of Kōgan warriors. Tokizawa drew on:

1. The teachings of Yagyū Sekishūsai.
2. Techniques and strategies devised by Munenori.
3. The methods of Munenori's son Yagyū Munefuyu (宗冬).
4. The tactical doctrine of the Shirin-dan.

He built a style that goes beyond Shinkage-ryū and named it Tenshinryu (天心流), "the style that follows the will of Ten." The transmission scrolls name Yagyū Munenori as the distant ancestor (遠祖, enso) and Tokizawa Yahei as the founder (流祖, ryūso).

## The Yagyū-gasa and the Aoi no Gomon

Two crests appear on the Tenshinryu flag.

- **The Aoi no Gomon (葵の御紋)** is the crest of the Tokugawa shogunate. Its use was strictly regulated. Tenshinryu uses the version from the early Edo period, up to the third shogun, because that is when Kōgan was active.
- **The Yagyū-gasa (柳生笠)** is the Yagyū clan crest. The Yagyū family's official crest is Waremokō-ni-Suzume (地楡に雀). After the Sakazaki Incident of 1616, Munenori received the Sakazaki family's Nigaigasa (二蓋笠, "two hats") crest as a secondary crest, and it became known as the Yagyū-gasa.

Kōgan samurai sent on missions to other domains carried a license tag called a **kansatsu** (鑑札). It was pentagonal like a shōgi piece, about three sun (9 cm) long, and bore the Aoi no Gomon, the kaō (seal) of Tokugawa Iemitsu, the Yagyū-gasa, and the kaō of Yagyū Munenori. A tag with the Aoi crest and the shogun's seal carried great authority, so it was made thin and easy to break and discard if it was ever at risk of being stolen or misused.

Tenshin Sensei spoke often of Yagyū Munenori and used the Yagyū-gasa on many occasions. Later, as he explained the school's history in more detail, the school officially adopted the Aoi no Gomon and the Yagyū-gasa as symbols of its deep connection to Kōgan. Their place on the flag expresses the aim of carrying that legacy into the modern age.

## Transmission to the present

| Generation | Name |
|---|---|
| Distant ancestor | Yagyū Munenori (柳生宗矩) |
| 1st (founder) | Tokizawa Yahei (時沢弥平) |
| 2nd | Kunitomi Chūzaemon (国富忠左衛門) |
| 3rd | Kunitomi Yazaemon (国富弥左衛門) |
| 4th | Kunitomi Yagozaemon (国富弥五左衛門) |
| 5th | Kunitomi Gorōzaemon (国富五郎右衛門) |
| 6th | Ishii Tsuneemon (石井恒衛門) |
| 7th | Ishii Benzō (石井辨蔵) |
| 8th | Ishii Seizō (石井清造) |
| 9th | Nakamura Tenshin (中村天心) |
| 10th | Kuwami Masakumo (鍬海政雲) |
| 11th | Ide Ryusetsu (井手柳雪) |

Several names were handed down through multiple generations, so the true number of generations may be higher. The lineage comes from Tenshin Sensei's recollection of the scroll manuscripts, and some details may be imperfect. The Ishii family kept the style alive through the Meiji period and the war years, and Ishii Seizō passed it to Nakamura Tenshin in the 20th century.

## Name

The school's registered name is **Edo Yagyū Tenshinryu Hyoho** (江戸柳生天心流兵法). Other groups that use the name Tenshinryu are unrelated.
