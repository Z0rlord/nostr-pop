# Spatial Terminology (Ten-Shin-Chi, In-Yō, Mei-An)

Tenshinryu keeps early-Edo and school-unique terms that modern Japanese alone will not decode; Kuwami Shike stresses they must be learned until responses are instant (Kuwami 2026, ch. "Regarding Archaic and Unique Terminology").

## The six directions

| Term | Japanese | Meaning | Literal sense |
|------|----------|---------|---------------|
| Ten | 天 | top / upper | heaven, sky |
| Shin | 真 | middle / center | center |
| Chi | 地 | bottom / lower | earth, ground |
| In | 陰 | right | shadowed, hidden side |
| Yō | 陽 | left | sun, brightness |
| Mei | 明 | front / forward | brightness, what is clear |
| An | 暗 | back / behind | darkness, what cannot be seen |

Note the school-specific mapping: **In (陰) is right, Yō (陽) is left**, the reverse of what many students first assume. These appear constantly in instruction, for example in [[reiho/okigatana]] placements (In/Yō/An no okigatana) and in the kami-no-ashi rule for [[reiho/zaho-seated-postures|Koza]].

## Nakazumi 中墨

The **centerline dividing left and right**. The term comes from traditional carpentry, where the carpenter marks a board's centerline with an ink pot (sumitsubo 墨壺) and inked string (suminawa 墨縄). Shintō and related traditions call the line **Seichū 正中**; many other martial arts say **Seichūsen 正中線**, a term not commonly used inside Tenshinryu.

## Jinchūro 人中路

The **centerline of the human body**, the Nakazumi applied to a person. The name derives from jinchū 人中 (also read ninchū), the vertical groove between the base of the nose and the upper lip.
