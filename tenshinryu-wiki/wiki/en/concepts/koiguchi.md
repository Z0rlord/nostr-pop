# Koiguchi 鯉口 — the Scabbard Mouth

The scabbard's opening, "carp's mouth" (also koikuchi, or sayaguchi 鞘口). A sheathed blade hangs suspended without touching the scabbard's inner surfaces, and the **habaki 鎺** (blade collar) fits the koiguchi tightly to keep the blade in: **"koiguchi ga katai" (a tight koiguchi)**. Drawing and sheathing wear the fit loose: **"koiguchi ga yurui"**, and the blade slipping out on its own is **sayabashiru 鞘走る** (Kuwami 2026, "The Koiguchi").

An everyday sword (sashiryō 差料, sashimae 差前, haitō 佩刀) had a **tight** koiguchi, so simply gripping the hilt would not free it. Drawing required **koiguchi wo kiru 鯉口を切る**: the left hand pushes the tsuba and moves the blade out about one sun (about 3 cm, roughly the habaki's length). A small movement that can decide life or death; "kiru" here means releasing the fit, not cutting anything.

## Three release methods

1. **Soto-giri 外切り / arawa-giri あらわ切り (outer, "visible" release):** the standard. Thumb against the tsuba, slightly toward the inside, never directly over the edge; the left hand stays close enough that its index side touches the tsuba, because force falls off with distance.
2. **Uchi-giri 内切り / kakushi-giri 隠切り (inner, "concealed" release):** the thumb pushes from **inside** the tsuba, hidden behind it, delaying the opponent's read; used for surprise, especially in seated techniques (zahō 坐法) and short-sword draws. A fist variant presses thumb and index together for very tight koiguchi, and a reverse-gripped scabbard can be released with the little-finger side of the hand.
3. **Hikae-giri 控切り (retaining release):** thumb and index both on the tsuba; after the one-sun release they **hold** the tsuba, keeping the blade half-freed and ready. This grip is **hikae-tsuba 控え鍔**, also used in nōtō. Not for sudden draws: for facing an opponent with the koiguchi released in advance, safe against jolts and against another person pulling the blade.

## Finger precautions

Whatever the method, as the release becomes the draw, **bend the thumb (and index, if used) against the scabbard**. Left extended, they can be cut if the tip's line changes (a struck hilt, a gust). Ishii Sensei called drawing with fingers left extended **"chī-chī-pappa"** and strictly warned against it.

For sayabiki, keep the web of the thumb-index slightly off the koiguchi rim and catch the pads' middles against it, drawing the scabbard back with the pads instead of a full grip. A beginner's left hand gripping far from the koiguchi usually means the release was never done; loose practice swords invite exactly this habit. Early on, chase correct sequence, not speed.

## Maintaining the koiguchi

Training swords wore simple mountings and ran loose by design; restraining the tsuba in [[reiho/sageto|sagetō]] grew from that, across many classical schools. Still, an over-worn koiguchi breaks and must be repaired periodically:

1. Cut a small piece of **kyōgi 経木** (thin wood sheet), glue it inside the scabbard mouth at the worn spot (rice paste historically; quick-drying wood glue today).
2. Do not overtighten, or the scabbard splits; file back any excess.
3. The wear pattern also reveals your own drawing habits.

Cellophane tape on the habaki is an emergency-only measure for a demonstration: it lets wear continue, damages the scabbard, and leaves residue; remove it and repair properly. This repair is for training and practical swords only, not valuable or historical blades.
