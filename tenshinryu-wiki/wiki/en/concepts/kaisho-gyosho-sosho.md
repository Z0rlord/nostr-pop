# Kaisho 楷書, Gyōsho 行書, Sōsho 草書 and Shu-Ha-Ri 守破離

Two threefold progressions in learning and performing technique (Kuwami 2026, "Kaisho, Gyōsho, and Sōsho", "Shu-Ha-Ri").

## Kaisho, gyōsho, sōsho

From calligraphy: **kaisho 楷書** (regular script, each stroke clear; also shinsho 真書, the root of the expression **shin-gyō-sō 真行草**), **gyōsho 行書** (semi-cursive), **sōsho 草書** (cursive). This is their learning order, not their historical order.

In Tenshinryu, **kaisho is the beginner's form** of a technique: movements clearly separated, or simplified for first learning. The same technique is then practiced in progressively combat-oriented **gyōsho** and **sōsho** forms. The distinction is about **how fully connected and adaptable the movements are**, not merely about performing them faster.

## Shu-ha-ri

Said to originate with Sen no Rikyū 千利休 and chanoyu 茶の湯, later adopted across the traditional arts:

- **Shu 守:** follow the teaching faithfully.
- **Ha 破:** individual character and originality take appropriate, effective form; departing from the set form becomes acceptable.
- **Ri 離:** even acting freely on one's own judgment, one no longer departs from the underlying principles; that is what makes moving beyond the explicit teaching possible.

The verse attributed to Rikyū:

> 規矩作法守り尽くして破るとも離るるとても本を忘るな
> Follow the rules and forms completely. Even if you break them or leave them behind, never forget their foundation.

This has also been read as guidance for **every day's practice**, not a decades-long ladder: tea is for today's guests; martial arts prepare for a confrontation that may come today or tomorrow. Tenshinryu values long training, but prizes practical effectiveness and adaptability in an immediately possible encounter. Kuwami notes the precise definition of shu-ha-ri within the school is not entirely settled for him; in one sense it is a broader progression encompassing kaisho-gyōsho-sōsho, and for convenience it also describes stages of training.
