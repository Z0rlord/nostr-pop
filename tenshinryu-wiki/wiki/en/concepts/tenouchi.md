# Tenouchi 手の内 — How the Hands Hold the Sword

Tenouchi ("inside the hand") is how the hands hold the sword together with how they function. The hands are the only connection between body and sword, so tenouchi is fundamental (Kuwami 2026, "Placing the Hands on the Sword Before Drawing", "How to Hold the Sword").

## Placing the hands before the draw — two beginner methods

1. **Gasshō-tsuka-kudashi 合掌柄下:** join the palms before the chest, then lower both hands, left to the scabbard, right to the hilt, **elbows kept against the ribs the whole way**, forearms sliding along the sides. Flared elbows or open armpits defeat the exercise.
2. **Shogaku-gasshō 初学合掌:** from lowered arms, bring the hands together as if to pray, left to scabbard, right to hilt, the right hand coming **beneath the wakizashi hilt**. Forearms stay on the ribs, as if gathering up hanging sleeves (tamoto 袂). Beginner faults: raised shoulders, flared elbows, right wrist bent inward; all telegraph the draw.

## The right hand

- At the **kaisho** stage, do not close around the hilt: four fingers together, hilt held lightly between thumb and index, the hand resting as softly as possible. It becomes **tatsu-no-kuchi 龍の口** ("dragon's mouth") as the draw proceeds.
- Once the light hold is reliable, the fingers bend naturally and rest individually against the hilt, approaching tatsu-no-kuchi from the start.
- At the kyū 急 (full-speed) stage, tenouchi forms naturally with consistent pressure.
- **Bōtō 防刀 variation:** in most defensive (and some offensive, kōtō 攻刀) techniques the hand lays **over the top of the hilt**, palm near horizontal; here the right elbow may leave the side.

## The left hand

Often neglected, yet in a sense **more important than the right**: most people control it worse and attend to it less. Expect to give it several times the right hand's attention.

- The left thumb pushes the tsuba to [[concepts/koiguchi|release the koiguchi]], never positioned directly above the edge.
- **Rotate the left forearm inward until the index knuckle is roughly above the cutting edge.** The forearm then lies near parallel to the scabbard, and the pull of the scabbard (sayabiki 鞘引き) aligns with the scabbard itself. Too little rotation flares the elbow and breaks the draw's line. Correct gasshō-tsuka-kudashi builds exactly this.

## The grip — tatsu-no-kuchi 龍の口

Both hands form tatsu-no-kuchi, a slightly relaxed [[concepts/hand-shapes|ika-no-kuchi]]: thumb, index and middle fingers separated from one another; middle, ring and little fingers stepped.

- **Right hand:** far enough from the tsuba that the index touches neither tsuba nor fuchigane 縁金; a hen's egg should fit in the gap (an actual egg was the old check). Hold the hilt **diagonally across the palm**, along the palmistry "life line", never near a right angle to the palm.
- **Left hand:** wraps the **tsukagashira 柄頭**, little finger completely beyond its end. Beginners can learn it by resting the pommel on the palm and closing the hand. This hand spacing gives leverage. When a technique needs rapid grip changes, the left hand shifts slightly toward the tsuba.

## Tachi grip

With a tachi 太刀, the right hand sits **even farther from the tsuba**: in armored combat (kaisha 介者) ranges close and tsubazeriai 鍔迫り合い is frequent, and a forward hand gets crushed between guards. The left hand shifts toward the tsuba, off the **kabutogane 兜金** (gripping it loosens the hilt wrapping; a fitted sarude 猿手 interferes). In combat the hands slide as needed (tsumezuka 詰柄, okurizuka 送柄), and a firm non-tatsu-no-kuchi grip is used for powerful blows and test cutting (shizan 試斬). Master the principles, then adapt the grip to the situation.
