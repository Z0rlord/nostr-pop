Yagyū Munenori and the Later Edo Yagyū Family
Before the Battle of Sekigahara*1 , Munenori cooperated with various lords in Yamato
Province to block the Western Army from the rear. During the battle itself, he served
at the Tokugawa main camp. As a result of his contributions, he was granted a
2,000-goku fief in Yagyū Village.
In 1601 (Keichō*2 6), Munenori was appointed as martial arts instructor to Tokugawa
Hidetada, the future second shogun.
Later, during the Summer Campaign of Osaka in 1615 (Keichō 20), Munenori is said
to have cut down seven hadaka musha (裸武者 A literal translation would be "naked
warrior.")—lightly armed or unarmored warriors, often considered suicide attackers
—who approached Hidetada*3 .

In 1616 (Genna*1 2), during the Sakazaki Incident(坂崎事件*2), Munenori was
responsible for negotiating and resolving the conflict. Following the incident, the
Yagyū family adopted the Sakazaki family crest (kamon 家紋), known as Nigaigasa
(二蓋笠, "two hats"), as their secondary crest (uramon 裏紋*3). This emblem later
became known as the “ Yagyū Kasa (柳生笠),” symbolizing the Yagyū lineage.
In 1621 (Genna 7), Munenori was appointed martial arts instructor(Hyoho
Shinanyaku兵法指南役) to Tokugawa Iemitsu (徳川 家光), who would later
become the third shogun. In 1632 (Kan'ei 9), he was named Sōmetsuke (総目付),
Inspector General of the Tokugawa shogunate.
*1 Genna 元和
One of Japan's era names. It followed the Keichō (慶長) era and preceded the Kan'ei (寛永) era,
spanning the years 1615 to 1624.
*2 Sakazaki Incident 坂崎事件 Also known as the "Senhime Incident ( 千姫事件 )," this event took
place after the Osaka Campaign (Osaka-no-Jin 大阪の陣 ) and involved a conflict between
Sakazaki Naomori ( 坂崎直
盛 ) and the Tokugawa shogunate, centered around Senhime ( 千姫 )—the granddaughter of
Tokugawa Ieyasu and wife of Toyotomi Hideyori ( 豊臣秀頼 ).
Naomori rescued Senhime during the Osaka Campaign and expected to marry her, but the promise
was broken, deeply wounding his pride. He then plotted to abduct her, but the plan was uncovered.
(Various theories exist.)
Munenori, acting as an emissary, went alone and unarmed to Naomori's residence to persuade him to
abandon the plot. Naomori is said to have committed seppuku (切腹 ritual suicide) in exchange for
the survival of his family, although some accounts suggest his retainers forced him to do so.
However, the shogunate did not honor the agreement, and the Sakazaki family of the Tsuwano

Domain (津和野藩) was abolished.
As a reward, Munenori received the Sakazaki estate in Fushimi (伏見), the family's weapons, and

the Nigaigasa (二蓋笠) family crest. He also took in Naomori's heir, Heishirō (平四郎), granted
him a 200-koku stipend, and arranged for him to live in Yagyū, Yamato Province. Additionally,
Munenori employed two of Sakazaki's former retainers.
*3 The Yagyū Family Crest
The official (jōmon 定紋 ) crest of the Yagyū family is known as Waremokō-ni-Suzume ( 地楡に雀
), depicting a sparrow (suzume) with the waremokō plant (Sanguisorba officinalis in English), a
perennial herb that grows in sunny grasslands.
The alternative crest (uramon 裏紋) is also referred to as a fukumon (副紋, secondary crest) or
kaemon (替紋, substitute crest).



By 1636 (Kan'ei*1 13 *6), through an increase in his stipend, Munenori had attained
the status of daimyō, receiving a 10,000-goku domain and establishing the Yagyū
Domain in Yamato Province. This marked an unprecedented rise for a swordsmanship
instructor to the rank of daimyō—a distinction unmatched before or since.
Munenori passed away in 1646 (Shōhō*2 3) at the age of 76.

After Munenori's death, the estate was divided between his eldest son, Yagyū Jūbei (
柳生 十兵衛), and his third son, Yagyū Munefuyu (柳生 宗冬*1), resulting in the
Yagyū family being downgraded from daimyō status to that of hatamoto (direct
retainers of the shōgun). Shortly thereafter, in 1650 (Keian*2 3), Jūbei passed away,
and Munefuyu succeeded as head of the Yagyū family and inherited its estates.
In 1668 (Kanbun 8), the domain's stipend was increased once more to 10,000 goku,
restoring the family's daimyō status. From that point onward, the Yagyū family
remained a daimyō house until the Meiji Restoration.

tortured them, with some being forced to commit seppuku.

*1 Sankin-kōtai 参勤交代
A system instituted by the Edo shogunate that required daimyō to alternate residence between Edo
and their home domains at regular intervals. Under this system, a daimyō would spend one year in
Edo, leaving his wife and children behind as virtual hostages when returning to his domain. After
spending a year in his domain, he would then return to Edo. This policy imposed heavy financial
burdens on the domains and, according to some theories, was intended to weaken their ability to
rebel. It served both as a means of political control and of reducing the daimyō's power within their
own territories.
*2 Sabakuha 佐幕派 Literally meaning “ supporters of the shogunate,” this term refers to factions
loyal to the Tokugawa regime. It is used in contrast to Tōbakuha (倒幕派, anti-shogunate factions)
and Kinōha (勤皇派, imperial loyalists).
*3 Kinōha 勤皇派
Refers to those who demonstrated unwavering loyalty to the emperor—particularly those involved
in the political ideologies and movements of the late Edo period aimed at overthrowing the
shogunate and restoring direct imperial rule.
