Family and Honor as Central Values
One of the defining characteristics of the samurai was the belief that one's family and
honor(Ie to Meiyo 家と名誉 ) were more important than one's own life.
Unlike the modern era, during the Edo period it was extremely difficult for
individuals to survive independently. It was a society in which people inevitably
depended on the groups they belonged to, both physically and mentally. Because of
this mutual interdependence, it was natural for the "ie" (家 household or family
lineage) to be valued above the individual. This prioritization of the family ensured its
survival as a basic strategy for continuing one's lineage.
At the same time, honor served as a significant social status, and to lose it was
equivalent to losing one's ability to survive. In a sense, honor was considered even
more important than life itself.
Japan is often described as a “ culture of shame,” and this reflects how seriously
honor was regarded. The widespread use of kamon (family crests) in Japan is also
symbolic of the cultural importance placed on family names. Damaging someone's
family crest was seen as an act that stripped them of their honor.
In Tenshinryu, there is a teaching that, when defeating an opponent, one might cut the
kamon from their haori(羽織 jacket) or kosode(小袖, kimono着物) and let them go.
This was seen as a grave disgrace to the family name, and it was said that the only
options left for the disgraced samurai were to commit seppuku (ritual suicide) or flee
into exile—shuppon (出奔, abandoning one's clan), also known as dappan (脱藩).
