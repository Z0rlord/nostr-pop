About the Lineage of Tenshinryu
In Tenshinryu, the successor (Sōdensha 相伝者) of the style is referred to as the
Shike (師家). This corresponds to what is commonly known today as the Sōke (宗家
). The term Shike originates from Buddhism—in particular, Zen Buddhism—where
the legitimate transmission of the Buddha's teachings is called Shihō (嗣法), and the
Zen master who receives this transmission is called the Shike. As martial arts were
heavily influenced by Buddhist culture, many styles adopted the term Shike during
the Edo period.
By the Shōwa period, however, this term had become less familiar to the general
public, and more styles began using the term Sōke for ease of understanding. Ishii
Sensei also used the term Sōke, and Tenshin Sensei followed suit. Shortly after I
joined the style, having previously studied Edo-period martial arts culture and
terminology, I mentioned to Tenshin Sensei, “ I've heard that the term Sōke was not
originally used in Edo-period martial arts.” He responded with great excitement, as if
he had finally found someone who truly understood him, and exclaimed, “ That's
right! In Tenshinryu, we actually call it Shike!”
From that point forward, Tenshinryu decided to revert to the original term, and we
have used only Shike ever since.

*1 Enso 遠祖
Enso refers to distant or ancestral predecessors. In the context of martial arts, traditional performing
arts, and other forms of traditional culture, it is used to indicate masters from whom the founder of a
style learned, as well as the founders of styles that served as the foundation for the current tradition.
*2 Shodai 初代
Shodai refers to the first generation in a lineage or system, as well as the individual holding that

position. It is also known as Ichidaime (一代目).
*3 Ryūso 流祖
Ryuso refers to the founder of a specific style. The word "Ryū" (流) comes from Ryūgi (流儀 –
style or method), and "So" (祖) means ancestor or founder—together denoting the originator of a
system.

*4 Kōso 高祖
Normally, this term refers to the great-grandparents or distant ancestors. In this context, it designates
the father of Tokizawa Yahei, the founder of the style. While the idea is somewhat nuanced, the use
of a name phonetically similar to Ryuso indicates that this figure holds considerable importance and
is therefore mentioned alongside the official founder.
*5 It is unclear how many generations carried the same name.

It appears that the name “ Tokizawa Yahei ” was inherited across multiple generations
using the same characters. However, it is unclear whether the second and later
generations of Tokizawa Yahei were formally part of the Tenshinryu lineage.
Similarly, the name “ Kunitomi Chūzaemon ” was inherited across multiple
generations. Because of this, Ishii Sensei noted that the actual number of generations
(with Ide Sensei being recorded as the 11th) may in fact be higher. (In other words, it
is believed that, in truth, Ide Sensei would be considered the 13th or 14th Shike.)
However, these details are based on Tenshin Sensei's recollections of the Densho(伝
書) manuscripts, and there is a possibility of memory errors. One reason Tenshin
Sensei was reluctant to disclose lineage information—even to his students—was his
concern that others might criticize or dispute such possible inaccuracies in memory.
