About Budō and Bujutsu
Budō (武道) is a modern collective term for martial arts such as jūdō, kendō, kyūdō
(archery), karatedō, aikidō, and jūkendō (bayonet fencing). The use of the suffix “-dō
” (meaning “ the Way ”) began after the Meiji Restoration. It was modeled after
traditional Japanese cultural arts such as kadō(華道 flower arrangement), sadō(茶道
tea ceremony), and kōdō(香道 incense appreciation), which also emphasize spiritual
cultivation.
This shift is said to have originated with Kano Jigoro, the founder of the Kōdōkan,
who reexamined the forms and systems of classical jūjutsu, renamed it jūdō, and
established a new approach.
In a similar manner, kenjutsu became kendō, and kyūjutsu became kyūdō. By
changing the character for “ technique ” (術 jutsu) to “ way ” (道 dō), the martial arts
were given an added moral and educational dimension—emphasizing not only
physical training (taiik 体育) but also moral development (tokuiku 徳育).
This transformation was a deliberate strategy to preserve martial traditions at a time
when the samurai class had been abolished and many martial arts were in decline.
During the Edo period, the term budō was primarily used to refer to bushidō (武士道
the way of the warrior).
For this reason, martial arts developed before the Meiji Restoration are sometimes
referred to as Kobudō (古武道 old budō), as if to distinguish them from the newer
budō.
However, when considering the chronological development of these terms, this usage
is technically inaccurate.
The modern term budō, as used today, is a newly coined word referring to martial arts
systems founded or restructured after the Meiji Restoration.
Therefore, adding the prefix “ ko-” (meaning “ old ”) to budō can be considered a
misapplication, at least in a historical sense.
For this reason, Ishii sensei does not favor the term kobudō (old martial ways), and
within Tenshinryu, the terms bujutsu (martial techniques) or bugei (martial arts) are
used instead.
When it is necessary to clearly distinguish these from modern budō, the terms koryū
bujutsu (古流武術 classical style martial techniques), koryū (古流



classical styles), or kobujutsu (古武術 old martial techniques) are used.
Some argue that the historical term kobujutsu did not exist during the Edo period, and
thus it should not be used at all (a point that could equally apply to kobudō). From the
standpoint of strict historical preservation, this argument certainly has merit.
However, in the modern day, how we express and communicate the nature of a
tradition is equally important in preserving it.
Using terminology that accurately reflects a system passed down continuously over
generations, and that helps distinguish it from others, is highly useful.
In this sense, even the term kobudō has some utility. However, out of respect for
Ishii-sensei's intentions, Tenshinryu deliberately avoids using it.
Moreover, since the 2000s, the term kobujutsu has become widely recognized in
Japan, but often in ways that are disconnected from its original, traditional context.
Although this brought a bit of attention to authentic classical styles, in the long run, it
did little to help preserve those traditions and instead led mainly to
misunderstandings.
No matter how widespread a term may become, unless its true substance is correctly
conveyed and it gains legitimate cultural recognition that contributes to the
preservation of tradition, it is ultimately meaningless.
Rather than being used solely for gaining attention, such terms must be properly used
and reclaimed by those responsible for their inheritance.
Needless to say, this sensitivity to terminology reflects the values and pride held
within Tenshinryu.
How other styles or organizations choose to use such terms is entirely their own
concern, and we ask that you refrain from criticizing them without cause.
