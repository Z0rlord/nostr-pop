Regarding Den-i
Most modern martial arts adopt the dan (段) and kyu (級) ranking system. It is said
that this system originated in the Meiji era, when Jigoro Kano(嘉納治五郎) —who
will be mentioned later—modeled it after the ranking systems used in Go(碁*1) and
Shōgi (将棋*2). In addition, many organizations use colored belts in conjunction with
the dan and kyu system, where practitioners progress through various belt colors from
white upward during the kyu levels, and wear a black belt (kuro-obi 黒帯) upon
reaching a dan rank (段).

In contrast, the ranking system used in martial arts during the Edo period is generally
referred to as Den-i (伝位*3). This system is primarily based on the issuance of
certificates or scrolls. While some traditional martial arts styles have adopted the
modern dan and kyu system, Tenshinryu does not use it. This is due

Ranking structures vary between styles, but Tenshinryu employs a seven-level
system:
Hatsu-yurushi (初許*1)
Naka-yurushi (中許*2)
Oku-yurushi (奥許*3)
Kirigami (切紙*4)
Mokuroku (目録*5)

*1 Hatsu-Yurushi 初許 Literally means "first ( 初 hatsu) authorization ( 許し yurushi)." In
traditional Japanese arts—including martial arts—it refers to an early stage license granted by a

teacher. While menkyo (license) can sometimes indicate full permission, in this case, it represents a
preliminary stage.
*2 Naka-Yurushi 中許 Literally means "intermediate ( 中 naka) authorization ( 許し yurushi)." It is
the second level of permission in Tenshinryu's ranking structure.
*3 Oku-Yurushi 奥許 Literally means "deep/advanced ( 奥 oku) authorization ( 許し yurushi)."
Oku implies "inner," "profound," or "reaching the depths." Historically, it also referred to the inner
chambers of a residence, particularly where women of high status lived, hence the modern term
"okusan" ( 奥さん ) meaning "wife." In Tenshinryu, the three stages from Hatsu-Yurushi to
Oku-Yurushi form the foundation. In some styles, Oku-Yurushi is considered the final level of
licensing.

*4 Kirigami 切紙 Literally means "cut ( 切り kiri) paper ( 紙 gami)." This license is generally
granted at an early or intermediate stage. The name is believed to come from the practice of cutting a
section from a longer scroll and presenting it. In Tenshinryu, after building a foundational structure
in the first three stages, Kirigami marks the beginning of more serious training.
*5 Mokuroku 目録
Mokuroku means "catalog" or "inventory." In many traditional styles, it is granted to those at an
intermediate or higher level. In Tenshinryu, it signifies that one has learned nearly all of the style's
techniques.

Since Kaiden is awarded to only one individual by the headmaster (Shike 師家), the
system effectively comprises six practical levels.
Such systems serve as guidelines for assessing stages of progress and skill
development in training. During the Edo period, very few styles employed ranking
systems in which numbers increased incrementally. However, systems that involve
increasing numbers or changing belt colors provide clear milestones and goals, which
are highly effective in boosting motivation and fostering a sense of accomplishment.
As a result, these systems—especially the dan-kyu ranking structure and colored belts
—spread rapidly after the Meiji period as practical tools for organizational growth
and student recruitment. However, there is a risk that these tools can become ends in
themselves, ultimately distorting the true purpose of training.
Tenshin Sensei was granted only the Sōden Inka (full transmission) by Ishii Sensei.
For various reasons—such as the difficulty of clearly distinguishing the levels of
transmission, challenges in recalling the full content of the tradition, the lack of
students who had advanced far enough to require such detailed instruction, and the
need for appropriate offerings (gratitude payments) in exchange for licensing—
Tenshin Sensei refrained from granting these intermediate licenses for

However, considering the importance of improving motivation and clarifying training
stages, I (Kuwami) determined that the formal granting of transmission levels and
licenses was essential. With Tenshin Sensei's approval and relying on his memory, I
established approximate criteria and have been granting Den-i since around 2015.
That said, directly applying the traditional rank system once used by the Kōgan(光願
) samurai to modern practitioners proved to be highly challenging. As a result,
Hatsu-Yurushi was granted only to two individuals: Takizawa Sensei and Arakawa
Sensei. Consequently, we are currently devising and testing a unique ranking system
that subdivides each level of Den-i into smaller, more accessible stages, making them
attainable through regular modern training.
In any case, acquiring Den-i is not an end in itself but merely a means. If the means
becomes the goal, it inevitably leads to what is commonly known as burnout. This
issue is not unique to either the dan-kyu or Den-i systems; the same applies to
demonstrations, competition performances, and matches. In Tenshinryu, we plan to
hold performance gatherings and tournaments as part of training—not only for Den-i
and public demonstrations—but these should be recognized as methods of practice,
not final objectives.
The dan-kyu system is widely recognized around the world as a ranking method in
martial arts, and there are those who argue that it should be introduced in Tenshinryu
as well. However, we take pride in our traditions, and our mission is to preserve that
heritage and maintain our distinctiveness. Therefore, as both Ishii Sensei and Tenshin
Sensei have consistently rejected it, Tenshinryu will not adopt the dan-kyu system
now or in the future.
