Regarding Shinkageryū
Tenshinryu is a branch of Shinkage-ryū (新陰流). Therefore, I will provide a basic
explanation of Shinkage-ryū here.
Shinkage-ryū is a style of swordsmanship
founded by the military strategist
Kamiizumi Nobutsuna (上泉信綱) in
the late Sengoku period. Kamiizumi
studied various styles, including Nen-ryū
(念流*1), Shinto-ryū (新当流 / 神道流
*2), and Kage-ryū (陰流*3), which are
considered the three great sources of
Hyohō (Hyoho sandai Genryū 兵法三
大源流). It is said that he particularly
studied under Aisu Ikōsai (愛洲 移香斎
), the founder of Kage-ryū, and received
its secret teachings. He named his style
Shinkage-ryu (新陰流) to signify its
development from Kage-ryū (陰流). The
"Shin (新)" in Shinkage-ryū means
"new," thus the name translates to "New
Kage-ryū."

*1 Nen-ryu 念流
A style of swordsmanship founded by Nenami Jion (念阿弥 慈恩) in the early Muromachi period
(1336–1573).
*2 Shinto-ryu 新当流 / 神道流
A general term for sword techniques passed down in the Kashima (鹿島) and Katori (香取) regions
of the Kanto area since the Muromachi period. It includes Kashima Shinto-ryu (鹿島新当流),
founded by Tsukahara Bokuden (塚原 卜伝), and is represented by Katori Shinto-ryu (香取神道
流), founded by Iizasa Chōisai (飯篠 長威斎).
*3 Kage-ryu 陰流
A style of swordsmanship founded by Aisu Ikosai (愛洲 移香斎) during the Muromachi period.

In 1563 (Eiroku*1 6), upon
learning that Kamiizumi would
visit Hōzōin in Nara, In-ei
informed Sekishūsai and invited
him to the temple. Sekishūsai
faced Kamiizumi in three matches
and lost all three. Impressed by
Kamiizumi's superior skill, he
became his disciple.
Afterward, Sekishūsai devoted
himself to training. In 1564
(Eiroku 7), when Kamiizumi
temporarily left Yagyū Village, he entrusted Sekishūsai with the development of
Mutōdori ( 無刀取り *2) techniques. Kamiizumi had been researching Mutōdori but
had not completed a satisfactory method. In 1565 (Eiroku 8), when Kamiizumi
returned, Sekishūsai demonstrated the Mutōdori techniques he had developed and was
awarded the Kaiden-Inka ( 皆伝印可 : full transmission license). In 1571 (Genki*3 2),
Sekishūsai received the "Ikkoku Ichinin" ( 一国一人 : One Country, One Master)
Inka, officially succeeding Shinkage-ryū.

Thereafter, Shinkage-ryū came to be known as Yagyūryū (柳生流) or Yagyū
Shinkage-ryū (柳生新陰流) and was passed down within the Yagyū family. The
lineage split into what is commonly referred to as Edo Yagyū (江戸柳生) and Owari
Yagyū (尾張柳生).

*1 Eiroku 永禄
A Japanese era name following Kōji (弘治) and preceding Genki (元亀), spanning 1558–1570.
*2 Mutōdori 無刀取り
Techniques used to confront and disarm an armed opponent while unarmed. "Mutō" means "without
a sword," and "tori" means "to take." In Tenshinryu, this is called "Dattōhō (奪刀法)," meaning
"the method of taking the sword."
*3 Genki 元亀
A Japanese era name following Eiroku (永禄) and preceding Tenshō (天正), spanning 1570–1573.



Owari*1 Yagyū, considered the true heir of Shinkage-ryū, was represented by Yagyū
Toshitoshi (柳生 利厳), martial arts instructor (Heihō Shihan兵法師範) to the
Owari Tokugawa family. Toshitoshi was the second son of Yagyū Toshikatsu (柳生
厳勝), the eldest son of Sekishūsai. The tradition continues today within the Owari
Yagyū family (Yagyū-kai 柳生会), as well as in organizations such as Marobashikai (
転会) and Shunpūkan (春風館).
Edo Yagyū was led by Yagyū Munenori (柳生 宗矩), who succeeded as family head
and served as swordsmanship instructor to the Tokugawa shogunate (Tokugawa
Shōgunke Kenjyutsu Shinanyaku徳川将軍家剣術指南役). Munenori, the fifth son
of Sekishūsai, was elevated to daimyo status in recognition of his achievements, and
the Yagyū Domain (柳生藩) was established in Yamato Province.

It was highly unusual for a sword instructor to become a daimyō. Munenori sent his
disciples across Japan to serve as instructors and also held the position of Sōmetsuke
(総目付). Consequently, fictional works often portray Munenori as secretly
supporting the Tokugawa regime, giving rise to the term "Ura Yagyū(裏柳生: Yagyū
in the Shadows)."
During the Edo period, when people referred to "Yagyūryū," they generally meant
Edo Yagyū, which had spread widely throughout Japan. However, after the Meiji
Restoration (明治維新), the Edo Yagyū family abandoned swordsmanship, and the
lineages of Edo Yagyū disappeared. It is said that the techniques of Edo Yagyū were
lost. Tenshinryu is one of the few—possibly the only—styles that traces its lineage to
Edo Yagyū and Yagyū Munenori.

This is a general overview of Shinkage-ryū. Since Tenshinryu is based on
Shinkage-ryū, it naturally shares many technical elements. However, there are also
significant differences, and it is not exactly the same as Shinkage-ryū*1 .

*1 Scrolls passed from Kamiizumi to Sekishūsai—and those authored by Sekishusai—show
significant changes in technique names and procedures. Furthermore, the scrolls Kamiizumi gave to
his various disciples varied in content depending on the recipient. At that time, such adaptations
were considered natural.
