About the Yagyū Family
The Yagyū clan, originally minor lords in Yamato Province, became widely known
during the era of Yagyū Muneyoshi (柳生 宗厳), also known as Yagyū Sekishūsai (
柳生 石舟斎). However, during Toyotomi Hideyoshi's(豊臣 秀吉*1 *1) Taikō Land
Survey*2 , the clan was reported for concealing rice fields. As a result, their 2,000-goku
domain was confiscated, and the Yagyū family fell into ruin in 1585 (Tenshō*3 13).
As a result, the young Munenori wandered across various provinces in search of a
retainer position. He even temporarily joined Hideyoshi's forces during the Siege of
Odawara. During these travels, Tokizawa Yahei, founder of Tenshinryu, accompanied
him. The Kyōdensho (教伝書, Tenshinryu Instructional Manual) contains anecdotes
from these journeys, as well as techniques that were developed during that time*4 .

*1 Toyotomi Hideyoshi (1537–1598)
A military commander of the Azuchi–Momoyama period (安土桃山時代), born in Owari Province.
He rose to prominence through military achievements while serving Oda Nobunaga. After
Nobunaga's death, he defeated Akechi Mitsuhide and went on to unify the country. He implemented

nationwide land surveys (kenchi 検地) and enforced the katanagari (刀狩)—the confiscation of
weapons from commoners—promoting the separation of warriors and farmers. Later, he sought to
conquer Ming China and launched an invasion of Korea, but died of illness during the campaign. He
also played an active role in the development of the tea ceremony, contributing significantly to the
flourishing of Momoyama culture.

*2 Land surveys (kenchi 検地) were carried out even before 1591 (Tenshō 19), the year when
Hideyoshi assumed the title of Taikō (太閤). However, all of these surveys are collectively referred
to as the Taikō-kenchi (太閤検地), or "Taikō land surveys." The title Taikō refers to a former
Sesshō (摂政, regent) or Kampaku (関白, chief advisor to the emperor) who has retired from
official duties.
*3 Tenshō 天正
Japanese era name from 1573 to 1592, following Genki and preceding Bunroku.
*4 Kyōdensho
Tenshinryu's oral and written transmission documents. These record the perspectives and travels of
Tokizawa Yahei, referring to Munenori respectfully as “ Waka (若, the Young Lord).”



In 1594 (Bunroku*1 3), Sekishūsai was invited by Tokugawa Ieyasu (徳川 家康 *2)
and traveled with Munenori to Shichiku Village (紫竹村) in Kyōto. At the time,
Ieyasu had been learning Shinkage-ryū (新陰流 or 神影流) from Okuyama Kyūgasai
(奥山休賀斎), but he took a particular interest in Sekishūsai's Mutōdori—a technique
to disarm an opponent bare-handed—which had gained wide acclaim.

In the presence of numerous military commanders, Sekishūsai demonstrated
Mutōdori. Ieyasu himself took up a wooden sword to experience the technique
firsthand. Deeply impressed by Sekishūsai's skill, Ieyasu expressed his admiration and
formally presented a written pledge on the spot, becoming Sekishūsai's disciple *3 . He
requested Sekishūsai to enter his service, but Sekishūsai, citing his advanced age,
declined. Instead, he recommended his son Munenori, who had accompanied him, and
Ieyasu accepted.
A further tale is preserved in the Tenshinryu oral tradition. After the outdoor
demonstration of Mutōdori, the group moved indoors. While seated, Ieyasu jokingly
said, “ Even the great Yagyū could not take the short sword at my side.” In that
moment, Sekishūsai threw a small metal clasp (kasugai 鎹) from his sleeve,
diverting everyone's attention. Seizing the moment, he used Miten (身転, a rolling
body movement akin to Ukemi 受身) to instantly close the distance and either seized
or touched Ieyasu's short sword. Amazed, Ieyasu is said to have gifted the short sword
to Sekishūsai on the spot.

*1 Bunroku 文禄
Japanese era name from 1592 to 1596, between Tenshō and Keichō.
*2 Tokugawa Ieyasu (1543–1616) Founder of the Edo shogunate. After aligning with Oda
Nobunaga and later with Toyotomi Hideyoshi, he ultimately seized power following the Battle of
Sekigahara in 1600. In 1603, he was appointed Sei-i Taishōgun, officially establishing the Tokugawa
regime.
*3 Ieyasu's pledge to Sekishūsai is regarded as a pivotal moment linking the Yagyū family to the
Tokugawa shogunate, resulting in Munenori's long and influential career in Edo.
