The crests of the Tokugawa family and the Yagyu
family, and the license tags (kansatsu)
About Family Crests (Kamon)
A family crest, or kamon (家紋), is an emblem passed down through generations to
represent a family's lineage, social status, or heritage.
The origin of family crests is believed to date back to the late Heian period, when
court nobles began marking their ox-drawn carriages with distinctive patterns to
easily identify their property. In samurai society, crests were used to signify the
lineage and affiliations of daimyō (大名, feudal lords) and hatamoto (旗本, direct
retainers of the shogun).
By the Edo period, the use of family crests had spread to commoners as well. The
total number of family crests used in Japan is not precisely known, but some
estimates suggest that there are over 25,000 unique designs.

About the license tags (kansatsu)
When assigned a mission to another domain, Kōgan samurai carried a Kansatsu



( 鑑札 )—a permit or license. A kansatsu was essentially what we would now refer to
as a certificate or official pass.
The kansatsu carried by Kōgan samurai was
pentagonal in shape, resembling a shogi (Japanese
chess) piece. Its exact dimensions are unknown,
but according to Tenshin-sensei, it was “ small
enough to fit in one's hand,” suggesting a size of
approximately three sun (about 9 cm).
These permits were similar in appearance to
modern-day souvenir tsūkō-tegata (通行手形,
travel passes) found throughout Japan.
The design of the kansatsu included several
elements: the Aoi no Gomon( 葵の御紋 ), the
crest of the
Tokugawa Shogunate, the kaō (花押, stylized signature or seal) of
Tokugawa Iemitsu, the Yagyū-gasa(柳生笠), the family crest of the
Yagyū clan, and the kaō of Yagyū Munenori.
