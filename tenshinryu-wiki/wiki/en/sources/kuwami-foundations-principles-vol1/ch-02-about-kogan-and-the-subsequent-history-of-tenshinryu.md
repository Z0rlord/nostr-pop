About Kōgan and the Subsequent History of Tenshinryu
In modern times, it is not widely known, but "Shirin" ( 士林 *1) is a term that refers
to a group of samurai. The name "Kōgan," ( 光願 ) used for the Shirin-dan( 士林団
), literally means "wishing for light. ( 光を願う Hikari wo negau)" Here, "light (
光 HIkari)" symbolizes peace, and the name was chosen with the hope ( 願 Gan,
Negau) that the long period of warfare would end, giving way to a peaceful era under
the Tokugawa shogunate.

To support their covert operations, Kōgan maintained a nationwide network of
collaborators. These individuals were referred to as Misshi (密士*1), meaning "secret
agents," and Sōshi (草士 *11*2), a term associated with operatives who often
disguised themselves among common people.
Kōgan was sometimes tasked with acting as forerunners (Sakibure 先触れ *12*3) for
the dismantling of feudal domains (Han 藩 ), conducting inspections of various
regional lords (Daimyō 大名 ), serving as bodyguards for high-ranking nobles*4 ,
carrying out sanctioned assassinations, and, on occasion, attending arbitrations related
to disputes over water rights or land boundaries*5 .

*1 Misshi 密士
Misshi is composed of mitsu (密, "secret") and shi (士, "samurai" or "warrior"). When the two kanji
are combined, the pronunciation shifts to misshi due to rendaku. Although the exact identity is
uncertain due to vague historical records, Tenshin Sensei indicated that this term may have referred
either to non-samurai collaborators or to Kōgan samurai themselves, when acting in covert roles.
*2 Soushi 草士 In Soushi (草士), the character sou (草) means "grass" and is often read as kusa
when used alone. While kusa usually means literal grass, it is also a slang term for a covert operative
or ninja, originating from the idea of lying hidden in fields or mountains to observe the enemy. Thus,
Soushi refers to informants, collaborators, or spies, who may or may not have held formal samurai
status but assisted in secret missions.
*3 Sakibure 先触れ Sakibure refers to forerunners or heralds dispatched to deliver advance
notifications of official actions. During such missions, it is said that Kōgan members wore haori

coats emblazoned with the Aoi no gomon ( 葵の御紋 ), the crest of the Tokugawa family,
symbolizing their authority and affiliation.

*4 It is said that Kōgan warriors also served as bodyguards for noble missions, including Kasuga no

*5 This refers to disputes over water rights and village boundary lines—matters that, in pre-modern

fatalities. Kōgan agents were occasionally dispatched to mediate or report on such conflicts as part

Through these diverse and often dangerous responsibilities, Kōgan played a key role
in supporting the Tokugawa shogunate. Their efforts helped establish and sustain an
era of peace and prosperity that lasted for over 260 years—one of the longest and
most stable periods in recorded human history.
However, ironically, the more peaceful the world became, the more Kōgan's role
diminished. During the reign of the fifth shōgun, Tsunayoshi (綱吉), significant
downsizing and personnel reductions were implemented. By the time of the eighth
shogun, Yoshimune (吉宗), Kōgan had been completely dismantled.
Some of its members were reassigned as Gokenin (御家人), while a few were
integrated into the Oniwaban (御庭番*1), the shogunate's covert intelligence service.
The Bakushi (幕士), who once had the honor of demonstrating their martial skills
before the third shogun, Iemitsu, considered it a profound humiliation to be denied
promotion to Hatamoto (旗本) and instead be given the lower-ranking status of
Gokenin (御家人).
Later, from the mid-Edo period onward, certain techniques of Tenshinryu were
transmitted in the Kansai region (関西*2), particularly within domains such as the
Mikazuki Domain (三日月藩, Mikazuki-han) and the Fukumoto Domain (福本藩,
Fukumoto-han). However, it is believed that the origins of these transmissions
remained obscured in the shadows of history and were never publicly acknowledged.
The Tenshinryu Kansai lineage scrolls discovered to date contain no information
regarding the style's origins, nor do they mention Yagyū Munenori or Tokizawa (the
founder). From the standpoint of maintaining a legitimate and continuous tradition,
omitting the name of the founder—arguably the most central figure—is highly
unnatural. It is likewise highly unusual from the perspectives of authority

Tenshinryu
Tenshinryu originated as a branch of Yagyū-ryū (柳生流), said to have been founded
by Tokizawa Yahei(時澤彌兵衛), a disciple of Yagyū Munenori. His own disciple,
Kunitomi Chūzaemon(國富忠左衛門), is credited with spreading the style.
Chūzaemon was a native of Bizen Province, and later entered the service of the
Matsudaira family of the Fukumoto Domain, where his name was passed down for
generations.
In this style, Omote ( 表 ) the upper level ( 上段 Jyodan) includes techniques such as
'Uke-Nagashi ( 受流し ),' 'Kiri-Chigai ( 切違 )', 'Cyudan-Koshi-Guruma ( 中段腰車
)', 'Jodan-Otoshi-Kiri ( 上段外し切 )', and 'Nyo-Setsu ( 怒切 )'. In the middle level,
there are techniques like 'Katage-Shinae ( かたげしなへ )', 'Kudari-Shinae( 下りし
なへ )', and 'Maruki-Bashi ( 丸木橋 )'. There are also traditions such as 'Aiki( 相氣
)', 'Aisen( 相先 )', 'Tsuri-Ai( 釣相 )', 'Suigetsu( 水月 )', 'Kanya-Shimo( 寒夜霜 )',
'Shinmyo-Ken( 神妙劍 )', and 'Kuru-ga-Gotoku( 來るが如く )'.
During the Kan'ei era, a practitioner named Watanabe Yuzaemon(渡邊友右衛門) is
said to have transmitted this style in Bizen. Additionally, a farmer from Hirai(平井),
who once served Kunitomi, learned this martial art and later returned to Bizen, where
he took the name Hirai Kanbei(平井勘兵衛) and is said to have become an instructor
of the style. It remains unclear whether this farmer and the aforementioned Watanabe
are the same individual or separate persons.*1

Includes Gekiken Sōdan (撃剣叢談). (Published in Taishō 12 [1923])

Gekiken Sōdan is a compilation by Mikami Gentatsu (三上元龍), a samurai of the
Okayama Domain, documenting various martial arts styles he observed between the
1760s and 1790s. It appears that a small amount of information regarding the founder
Tokizawa and Yagyū Munenori was preserved—most likely through oral tradition.
When we discovered the densho (伝書) of the Kansai-line Tenshinryu, we had hoped
they would provide evidence supporting the origins of Tenshinryu as taught



by Tenshin Sensei. However, as noted earlier, these books (and scrolls) contain no
mention of the lineage or the style's establishment. Given the historical context and
manner in which they were compiled, it is perhaps unsurprising that details
concerning the founder, the lineage, and other background information were omitted.
The Tenshinryu we practice today has been passed down from Ishii Shinemon
Yasuhiro (石井新右衛門安広*1), one of the first Kumigashira of Kōgan, to his
descendants, and later inherited by the Ishii family*2 , who served the Odawara
Domain. Even after the dissolution of Kōgan, the Ishii family and their associates
preserved Tenshinryu in secret, holding onto their sword techniques in the hope that,
should the need arise, they could once again serve with loyalty as retainers of the
Tokugawa shogunate.
Some among them joined the Shōgitai (彰義隊) during the late Edo period. Needless
to say, however, the age no longer called for swordsmanship, and they were ultimately
defeated by the superior firepower of Western-style firearms.
