Daidogei, or Japanese street performance, is a traditional form of entertainment in which artists
perform in public spaces such as streets, parks, and plazas. During the Edo period, it was also known
as Tsujigei (辻芸), where tsuji (辻) refers to an intersection or roadside, and gei (芸) means
performance. These performances, taking place at crossroads or along roadsides, came to be called

Tsujigei. Another term was Tezuma (手妻), referring to traditional Japanese magic tricks. The word
is believed to originate from the expression “ hands (te 手) moving as swiftly as lightning (inazuma
稲妻).” Members of the Kōgan group sometimes disguised themselves as traveling street performers
when acting as spies, and these skills proved useful during their missions.

Not only was the teaching of Tenshinryu prohibited by government order, but the
instructional books (Kyodensho 教伝書) that served as formal proof of transmission,
along with the writings of Ishii Sensei and important personal notes, were all lost.
This tragic event led Tenshin Sensei to develop a strong stance against smoking.

Eventually, fortune shone upon the disheartened Tenshin Sensei. While performing
Daidōgei, he met a woman working at the Indonesian embassy. They began a
relationship and were happily married. The Nakamura family was blessed with three
children. Although Tenshin Sensei had held various jobs up to that point, he
eventually secured stable employment at the post office. While he continued to face
adversity as a martial artist, he found peace and happiness as a family man.
Sadly, this happiness was short-lived. His wife passed away at a young age due to
laryngeal cancer. Seeking solace, Tenshin Sensei spent his days off performing
Daidōgei. Although Tenshin Sensei believed that teaching Tenshinryu was still
prohibited, he continued to pass it on privately to his children, colleagues, and a few
close acquaintances while raising his children alone. Since public demonstrations
were not banned, he also actively participated in them. An avid performer, Tenshin
Sensei eagerly took part in demonstrations (Enbu 演武) whenever the opportunity
arose. By the early 2000s, he was performing in demonstrations nearly once a month.
According to one of his students from that period, there were more demonstrations
than actual practice sessions.
With his retirement from the post office and his three children grown, Tenshin Sensei
decided around 2007 to begin teaching Tenshinryu to those outside his family. Nearly
fifty years had passed since the police had ordered him not to teach, and he held faint
hopes that either the statute of limitations had expired or that the authorities might
overlook his renewed instruction.
However, very few new students ever came. Not surprising, really—he had done
absolutely no promotion out of fear that it might be considered illegal. The few
students he already had failed to progress in the way Tenshin Sensei had hoped,



and his motivation to teach declined with each passing day.
Just as he was on the verge of giving up, on March 1, 2008, I (Kuwami Masakumo)
