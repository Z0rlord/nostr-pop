"Katsujinken" and "Setsunintō"
Katsujinken (also known as Katsuninken, 活人剣 – “ the life-giving sword ”) and
Setsunintō (殺人刀 – “ the death-dealing blade ”) are terms derived from Zen
expressions found in classic texts such as The Blue Cliff Record (Hekiganroku碧巌
録*1) and The Gateless Gate (Mumonkan無門関*2).
In the Shinkage-ryū tradition, Setsunintō refers to “ using a stance ” or “ controlling
the enemy through aggressive attack,” while Katsujinken refers to “ not using a stance
” or “ controlling the enemy through feints, setups, and subtle manipulation.” Yagyū
Munenori reinterpreted this concept as The General's Sword (Taishō no Ken 大将の
剣 ), a form appropriate for the shogunate that governed the nation.
Munenori left behind an important and widely respected text for future generations
titled Heihō Kadensho (兵法家伝書). At the beginning of this work, he writes:
"The contents recorded in these three volumes were originally meant to be seen
only by my family and were not to be shown to outsiders. However, the Way
should not be kept secret. Secrecy exists to make things known. If something is
truly kept secret, it is as if it does not exist at all. My descendants must deeply
consider and understand this meaning."
I would like you to keep these words in mind. Fortunately, this book is now
commercially available and accessible to anyone. If you have the opportunity, I highly
recommend reading it.
Now, I will share an excerpt from Heihō Kadensho that clearly explains the principles
of Katsujinken and Setsunintō:

*1 Hekiganroku 碧巌録
A classical text of Zen Buddhism compiled during the Song dynasty in China (960–1279). It
consists of one hundred kōan (公案)—Zen riddles or dialogues used for meditation and spiritual
training.
*2 Mumonkan 無門関
A classical Zen Buddhist text compiled in 1128 during the Southern Song dynasty in China. It
contains forty-eight kōan (公案).



There is an old saying:
"Soldiers are ominous by nature, and the Way of Heaven detests them. Yet, to
use them when there is no other choice—that too is the Way of Heaven."
What this means for a warrior is that weapons such as bows, swords, and
naginata are, in a sense, one's own soldiers—and thus can also be considered
ominous things.
Because the Way of Heaven is originally a path of nurturing and sustaining life.
To use weapons to kill is thus a great folly and a violation of this principle.
Therefore, such actions stand in opposition to the Way of Heaven and should be
avoided.
Yet, it is also said that in unavoidable circumstances, to kill may itself be in
accord with the Way of Heaven.
If killing one evil person allows ten thousand to live, then is not a sword that
takes life also, in truth, a sword that gives life?
Even in the use of weapons, there is a correct path. Without understanding it,
one who seeks to kill may be killed instead.
To view martial arts solely as a means of defeating others is a shallow
understanding.
The true goal is not to cut people down with a sword, but to cut down evil itself.
There is profound wisdom in the idea that taking the life of a single wrongdoer
may preserve the lives of many.”
Simply put, a sword used to pursue selfish desires is called Setsunintō (殺人刀, the
death-dealing blade)—a sword that harms others recklessly and strays from the Way
of Heaven.
On the other hand, even if a sword takes a life, if it does so for the sake of the world
and allows many others to live, it becomes Katsujinken (活人剣, the life-giving
sword).
As a martial art studied by the shogun who ruled the realm, the Yagyū family's
Shinkage-ryū emphasized that it must not be a petty or narrow form of martial
practice. Instead, it must embody the General's Sword (Taishō no Ken), a sword that
brings conflict to an end and governs the nation.
This philosophy of Katsujinken, as redefined by Yagyū Munenori, is deeply
embedded in the teachings of Tenshinryu.
Munenori's concept of Katsujinken had a profound impact on later generations. In



modern times, a sword that harms others is often referred to as Satsujinken (殺人剣,
the killing sword), while one that refrains from harm and serves society and its people
is called Katsujinken (the life-giving sword).
However, as seen in the teachings of Heihō Kadensho, Katsujinken is not about
naively or passively avoiding harm.
Even during the peaceful Edo period under the Tokugawa shogunate, martial arts—
and at times even war—were still necessary to suppress evil when no other means
remained.
Tenshinryu inherits this philosophy of Katsujinken, striving to resolve conflict
without drawing the sword whenever possible. If drawing the sword is unavoidable, it
should ideally be used with the back of the blade (mineuchi 峰打ち ) to avoid fatal
harm.
Even when a cut must be delivered, it should aim to inflict the lightest possible injury,
avoiding death and minimizing suffering.
Even if the opponent is an evildoer, they still have family, friends, and a life. It is
important to avoid creating deep resentment, to prevent prolonged or escalating
conflict, and to act with the skill and compassion required to achieve that outcome.
There are situations in which one may be ordered by a superior to take a life.
However, in some cases, rather than killing the target directly, one might cut off their
topknot or destroy their family crest, delivering a sentence in symbolic form. A
disgraced samurai, having suffered such humiliation, would be expected to commit
seppuku (ritual suicide) to preserve their honor.
There were also instances where a target's throat might be cut to silence them, their
eyes slashed to blind them, or their nose or ears severed as a public warning
(miseshime, 見せしめ —exemplary punishment).
To modern sensibilities, these acts may seem cruel. Yet even such measures were, in
their historical context, considered part of Katsujinken.
Ultimately, Tenshinryu is not merely a collection of techniques, but a strategic system
for governance. In this sense, everything within Tenshinryu embodies the spirit and
purpose of Katsujinken.
