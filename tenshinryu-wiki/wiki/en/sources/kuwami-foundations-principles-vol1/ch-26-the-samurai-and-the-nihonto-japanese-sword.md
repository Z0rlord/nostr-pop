The Samurai and the Nihontō (Japanese Sword)
The sword is the soul of the samurai.
( 刀は武士の魂 Katana ha Bsuhi no Tamashī)
In Tenshinryu, it is also taught that the sword is an extension of oneself.
Thus, the tradition refers to one's own body as the “ Kenshin ” (剣身 sword-body),
a unique term expressing this philosophy.
In Japan, the history of swords began with the Taurugi (剣)—a straight,
double-edged blade with no curvature. From ancient times, swords were not only
weapons but also venerated as sacred ritual objects.
At the same time, swords have long been regarded as works of art.
Having held aesthetic, ritual, and martial significance over the centuries, swords have
continued to captivate people through multiple dimensions.

Hassokudai 八足台 (right) and Sanpō 三方 (left)

For Tenshinryu, how one perceives and relates to the sword is of great importance.



In many martial traditions, sword etiquette (Tōrei 刀礼) is observed before and after
practice.
Typically, the practitioner sits in seiza and places the sword horizontally in front of
them, performing a seated bow.
This format is said to have become somewhat standardized among various styles after
the Meiji era.
Tenshinryu also preserves the tradition of sword etiquette, but with its own unique
form.
In formal rituals, one sits and places a stand*1 in front of them, respectfully offering
the sword upon it before performing a Shintō-style bow.
If no stand is available, the practitioner presents the sword with both hands and bows
the head.
Depending on the location, standing bows (ritsurei 立礼) may also be performed in
the same manner.

Standing-style Tōrei (刀礼)

案 offering stand or table). In some cases, other types such as sanpō (三方 three-sided tray) or

In our sitting method (zaho 坐法), placing the sword to the right, left, or rear aligns
with traditional samurai etiquette based on the concept of yin and yang. However,
placing the sword directly in front is not commonly done in samurai ceremonial
etiquette. Especially when performing rituals before a shrine, placing the sword
directly on the floor is considered improper.*1

Tenshinryu uses this form of sword etiquette primarily during special rituals, such as
when offering the sword before a sacred altar.*2
Equally important is the mindset with which the ritual is performed.
In Tenshinryu, when performing tōrei, we offer the sword we have been granted,
reflect inwardly on the meaning of having received it, and renew our vow never to use
the sword or martial skills for selfish desires, but only for rightful and public
purposes.
According to the teachings of Tenshinryu, the sword we wear and wield at our waist is
never truly a personal possession.
Of course, unless it is borrowed, it is usually purchased by the practitioner, and legal
ownership belongs to them.
However, what is being explained here transcends legal ownership.
This is not something limited to Tenshinryu or Kōgan, but rather a fundamental
principle behind why samurai of shibun (warrior status) were permitted to carry the
daishō—the long and short swords.
In essence, the daishō were bestowed by the eight million gods (yaoyorozu no kami)
of Japan, to protect and serve one's lord, domain, subjects, and above all, one's family
and household.
These swords were intended to be used for the public good—not as personal tools for
fulfilling private ambitions.

directly on the floor. They are typically set on an elevated platform. If they must be placed on the

At the heart of Tenshinryu's strict prohibition of “ private duels ” (shitō 私闘) lies
this very principle.
The sword etiquette practiced in Tenshinryu is a solemn ritual to reflect upon this
spirit, offer the sword before the divine, and renew one's oath.
While this is a teaching passed down in Tenshinryu, it is not a uniquely Tenshinryu
concept, but rather one of the core ideals of the samurai class itself.
Swords are not limited to one generation; if treated with care, they can be passed
down for many generations.
Even in modern practice, most practitioners use imitation swords (mozōtō), but as
long as they are shaped like swords, they too represent the spirit and soul of the
practitioner.
These should be regarded as shared cultural assets to be treated with care and respect.
Naturally, as training tools, they will experience wear and damage—avoiding all
damage would make proper training impossible.
In recent times, due to a certain degree of overemphasis, there has been a tendency to
overly sanctify Japanese swords, to the point where their role as weapons is often
downplayed, disregarded, or even forgotten.
However, the function of the sword as a weapon is an essential aspect that must not be
ignored.
It is incorrect to discuss swords solely from the perspective of sacred ritual objects or
art pieces.
At the same time, it is equally mistaken to carry the sword purely as a weapon without
reflecting on its sacredness and spiritual significance.
What is important is balance: to never lose sight of the multifaceted value of the
sword, to treat it with care and respect, and at the same time, to train diligently
without hesitation to let it wear when necessary.
