About Bushidō
Bushidō ( 武士道 ) refers to the code of conduct for bushi and samurai, and in earlier
times it was expressed through various terms such as "shidō 士道 " (the way of the
warrior), "budō武道 " (the martial way), "otoko no michi 男の道 " (the path of
manhood, otoko michi 男道 ), "samuraidō 侍道 " (the way of the samurai), or
"yumiya toru mi no narai 弓矢取る身の倣い " (the customs of one who takes up
bow and arrow), and "bu no michi 武の道 " (the martial path).
Since the Meiji era, especially under the influence of Nitobe Inazō's writings*1 , the
term "Bushidō" became fixed and standardized.
Needless to say, not all warriors of the past were ideal embodiments of Bushidō.
But precisely because of this, ideals such as "This is
how a samurai should be" were continuously taught
and upheld.
Of course, the ethical standards of the past do not
necessarily apply directly to modern life.
Norms and the ideal way to live differ depending on
the times.
Furthermore, Tenshinryu is a martial tradition, not a
manual for Bushidō.

students held a certain sense of aversion toward
Bushidō.

This was the reason why Tenshin Sensei and others came to distance themselves, to

However, through many years of studying under Tenshin Sensei, I came to clearly
understand that the importance of Bushidō is explicitly conveyed in the teachings of
Tenshinryu and in the words of Ishii Sensei. It became evident that, at its foundation,
Tenshinryu is imbued with the spirit of the samurai.
Bushidō is a prerequisite for strategy (heihō), and cannot be separated from it.
Without understanding the way of the warrior and how they lived, one cannot truly
understand or master Tenshinryu.
For that reason, I will briefly explain the universal idea of Bushidō here.
But it must be understood that the form of the samurai has changed throughout
history, and Bushidō is not an absolute or unchanging concept.
It is also vastly different from modern value systems and is not necessarily something
to be praised without question.
Be careful not to become self-righteous or exclusionary in its name.
It is important not to brandish Bushidō recklessly, nor to mock or belittle it. Only by
integrating it into one's own way of life does learning traditional martial arts have real
meaning.
In the martial arts world, it is widely claimed that training fosters character and
cultivates virtue—but this is a myth.
As early as the Meiji era, educators pointed out that martial arts training could
increase aggression and produce individuals who ignore those around them.
Due to this, mandatory martial arts classes in schools were once postponed.*1
This is closely related to the often-misused phrase, “ A sound mind in a sound body.”*2
Since martial arts involve learning techniques for conflict, they bring both positive
aspects to health and mentality, and certain negative effects as well.
That is precisely why we must not rely on martial arts alone to develop character.
Rather, because we study martial arts, we must make conscious efforts to cultivate
and refine our humanity.
As one guiding principle, we must not treat Bushidō lightly.
That is extremely important.
