About "Bushi" and "Samurai"
It is quite difficult to provide a strict and definitive explanation of the terms "bushi (
武士)" and "samurai (侍)."
Ishii-sensei preferred the use of the term bushi and taught Tenshin-sensei that
"samurai refers to a lower class."
However, this interpretation differs from established academic theories.
Historically, samurai was a term used for high-ranking warriors, and during the Edo
period, it became a general term applied to the warrior class as a whole.
The word is derived from saburau ( さぶらう ), meaning "to serve beside a noble
person." Over time, this evolved into samurai.
On the other hand, bushi originally referred to those who practiced martial skills
(bugei), and gradually came to be used to describe soldiers.
In the Edo period, both terms—bushi and samurai—were used to refer to individuals
of the warrior (shibun) class. At the very least, there was no clear social hierarchy
implied between the two terms.*1
Today, however, Tenshinryu as a rule uses the term bushi when referring to
individuals of warrior status.
While Ishii-sensei's statement may be academically disputable, what we value most is
the tradition and teaching of the style.
There may have been specific intentions or historical and cultural reasoning behind
his choice of terminology.
For us to casually alter that usage would risk distorting the tradition itself.
This principle applies not only to terminology but also to technique.
To change a transmission just because it differs from our own experience, from
common sense, or from academic consensus, would immediately lead to the tradition
becoming distorted, and ultimately, hollowed out.

*1 The issue of the origin and definition of this term remains, like the emergence of the bushi as a
social class, one of the more complex challenges in the field of historical and cultural studies.
