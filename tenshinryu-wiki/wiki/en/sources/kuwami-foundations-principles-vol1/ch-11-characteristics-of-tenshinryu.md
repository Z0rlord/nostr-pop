Characteristics of Tenshinryu
Below is a text that I slightly modified from the introduction of the Tenshinryu
originally written by Tenshin Sensei and published in the first edition of the
Tenshinryu Hyoho Beginner's Manual in 2012. (The original text was somewhat
awkward in Japanese, so I made adjustments for clarity.)
Tenshinryu Hyoho is a comprehensive martial art encompassing kenjutsu
(swordsmanship), battōjutsu (sword drawing techniques), sōjutsu (spear
techniques), naginatajutsu (glaive techniques), kusarigamajutsu (chain and
sickle techniques), and jūjutsu (grappling techniques). It also includes
jūmonji-sōjutsu (cross-shaped spear techniques) as an auxiliary tradition. Since
the time of its founding, practitioners have worn a kodachi (short sword) during
training, and the system preserves traditional battlefield

techniques using the tachi (long sword), referred to as Koshiki (ancient forms).
Not only does it transmit a wide range of weapon techniques, but it also instills a
philosophy known as Denchū-Tōhō Saya-no-uchi( 殿中刀法鞘ノ中 ), which
emphasizes resolving confrontations without drawing the sword. The art
includes extensive techniques and strategies for all aspects of a samurai's life—
lying down, rising, sitting, standing, walking, and running. It is a rare style that
preserves the etiquette, manners, and terminology of the samurai, passing them
down to the present day.
Certainly, the introduction captures the characteristics of Tenshinryu well. However, it
largely omits the historical aspects. As already mentioned, this was because Ishii
Sensei had instructed that details about himself be kept private. Now then, Ishii Sensei
was said to have repeatedly described Tenshinryu as "a style different from others."
(「他と異なる流だ」Tato inaru Ryu da)
What exactly was he referring to when he said it was different?
As noted earlier, older martial arts styles tend to be more comprehensive. In the Edo
period, martial arts styles became increasingly specialized, focusing on particular
disciplines such as kenjutsu, iaijutsu (sword drawing), jujutsu, or sojutsu. Many of
the styles that spread widely during the Meiji and Showa periods were highly
specialized systems that had emerged after the mid-Edo period. From that perspective,
Tenshinryu's comprehensiveness is certainly one of its defining characteristics.
However, Ishii Sensei's true meaning likely lay elsewhere. He was probably referring
to Tenshinryu's most significant distinction: its purpose. Most martial arts styles are
created after the founder has mastered a system, incorporating personal innovations or
creating new techniques based on their own experiences. Their aims are often to
demonstrate individual skill, gain recognition, or achieve personal advancement. In
this sense, the origins of many martial arts are fundamentally self-serving.
Tenshinryu, on the other hand, has very different origins. It was developed by and for
Kōgan, an organization charged with protecting the Tokugawa shogunate
(Ryūeigoji柳営護持). Its creation was not the result of a single founder's personal
ambitions, but rather a form of military training for public service. As such, it
possesses an exceptionally high level of public utility. It is a martial art created for
the nation's sake—a true Katsujinken.
