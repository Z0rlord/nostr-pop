The Five Constant Virtues: Jin, Gi, Rei, Chi, Shin
A bushi is, in essence, a warrior. After the country was unified, the bushi class
emerged as a new status to take over the responsibilities of warfare previously held by
the aristocracy.*1 Later, the warrior class also came to govern, but at its core, it
remained the path of archery and horsemanship (Kyūba no Michi 弓馬の道 ) —
that is, the way of the warrior. Bushi fought for their lord, for their land and people,
and above all, for their family's name and honor. It was their profession.
In the early days, the samurai primarily valued raw strength above all else. However,
as the years passed through the Kamakura, Muromachi, and Edo periods, the nature of
the samurai evolved.
As they came to serve as rulers and
governors, their role was reexamined,
refined, and gradually systematized.
As warriors and political leaders, bushi were
expected to fulfill their duties with high
moral standards. To cultivate such virtues,
the Tokugawa shogunate emphasized
Neo-Confucianism ( Shushigaku 朱 子 学 )
, based on Confucian teachings. Ancient
Chinese classics known as the Four
Books(Shisho四書) and Five Classics
(Gkyō 五経*2) were highly regarded, and
among their representative ideas was the set
of five virtues called the "Five Constant
Virtues"
(Gojō no toku 五常の徳 ): Jin (benevolence), Gi (righteousness), Rei (propriety),
Chi (wisdom), and Shin (faithfulness).
*1 It is said that the origin of the samurai class can be traced back to nobles who, among the
aristocracy, were particularly expected to maintain military preparedness.
*2 A collective term in Confucianism referring to the Four Books (the Great Learning, the Doctrine
of the Mean, the Analects, and Mencius) and the Five Classics (the Book of Changes, the Book of
Documents, the Book of Songs, the Book of Rites, and the Spring and Autumn Annals), which are
especially revered texts.



Jin (仁) – Compassion and consideration for others
Gi (義) – Justice, selflessness, and moral conduct
Rei (礼) – Traditional etiquette and decorum
Chi (智) – Understanding of reason and wisdom
Shin (信) – Trustworthiness and sincerity
It was considered essential to practice all of these virtues without neglecting any. The
number of pleats on the front of a warrior's hakama (traditional trousers) matched the
number of these virtues. Since wearing a hakama was a privilege exclusive to
warriors, it was said that the hakama symbolized their virtues.
Summarizing these virtues results in the following ideal image:
"One who possesses compassion for others, values justice and morality, honors
tradition and manners, seeks the proper form of all things with wisdom, and
earns the sincere trust of others."
When expressed in such simple terms, the concept does not appear particularly
difficult. However, when one considers how each of these virtues is to be manifested
through behavior and decision-making, especially in concrete cases, things become
more complex.
Yagyū Sekishūsai wrote the following in his Hyōhō hyakushu (兵法百首 One
Hundred Poems on Strategy):
「兵法の極意に仁·義·礼·智·信たへずたしなみ機遣（気遣い）をせよ」 "The

essence of strategy lies in jin (benevolence), gi (righteousness), rei (courtesy),
chi (wisdom), and shin (sincerity).
Always cultivate these virtues and be considerate of those around you."

「つねづねに五常の心（仁·義·礼·智·信）なき人に家宝の兵法印可ゆるすな」

"Do not grant the treasured license of martial strategy to anyone who does not
always possess the spirit of the Five Constant Virtues."

That a renowned strategist such as Sekishūsai passed down these teachings shows that
one's way of life was of utmost importance for both martial strategists and warriors.
