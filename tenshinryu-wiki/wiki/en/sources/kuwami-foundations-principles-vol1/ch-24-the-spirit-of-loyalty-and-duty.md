The Spirit of Loyalty and Duty
In the previous section, we noted that the front pleats of the hakama represent the Five
Constant Virtues (gojō no toku). In contrast, Tenshinryu teaches that the single pleat
in the back of the hakama signifies having "no double-mindedness (Nishin naki
koto wo Shimesu 二心なきことを示す )"—a state of undivided loyalty. This
focused state of mind is the very spirit of chūgi ( 忠義 ), or loyalty and duty.
It is often said of the Sengoku period that, “ When the tide of battle turns, it was
common practice to betray one's lord and defect to the enemy.” Indeed, the custom of
gekokujō (下剋上 lower ranks overthrowing their superiors) is also known as a
hallmark of that era.
Certainly, it is true that betrayals and acts of disloyalty were rampant during those
turbulent times. However, it is equally true that, even then—as in the Edo period—
loyalty and duty were esteemed as noble virtues.
To serve one's lord with sincerity and to fulfill one's responsibilities for the sake of
one's lord—this is the essence of the spirit of chūgi.
The act of committing Oibara wo kiru ( 追腹を切る following one's lord in death
through seppuku*1) and being praised as a chūshin (loyal retainer) was not limited to
the Edo period.*2
Especially between lords and their personal attendants (kinjū), deep emotional bonds
were often observed, sometimes even taking the form of romantic or shudō
(male-male affection) relationships. These ties ran deep.
On the battlefield, where stones and arrows flew and death was ever-present, to
entrust one's back to another required that level of trust. It is easy to imagine how
essential that bond of faith truly was.

*1 In contrast to Oibara (追腹, following one's lord in death through seppuku), sakibara (先腹)
refers to the act of committing seppuku before the lord's death.
*2 Due to the widespread practice of Oibara, the Tokugawa Shogunate viewed it as problematic and
officially banned it in May of Kanbun 3 (1663).
