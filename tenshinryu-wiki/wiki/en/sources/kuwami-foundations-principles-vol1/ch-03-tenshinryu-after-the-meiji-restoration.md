Tenshinryu after the Meiji Restoration
The Ishii family sought to revive Tenshinryu, and in 1887 (Meiji 20*1), Ishii Benzō (
石井辯蔵 ) Sensei attempted to establish a dojo in Shibahama, though the effort was
ultimately unsuccessful. His fourth son, Ishii Seizō ( 石井清造 ) Sensei (born in
1887), learned Tenshinryu from his grandfather, Ishii Tsuneemon ( 石井恒エ衛門 )
Sensei, along with descendants of the former Kōgan members—or possibly Soushi(
草士 ).
In 1912 (Taishō 1), Ishii Seizō Sensei was entrusted with the formal succession of the
style. However, amid the rise of militarism, the martial traditions of the Edo-period
samurai were no longer favored by the times, and efforts to popularize the art proved
unsuccessful. His son showed no interest in inheriting the style,

Initially, Ishii Sensei shared little about the history of the style but told his new
student, “ I am going to teach you something extraordinary.” From that point, he
began teaching Tenshinryu within Kengikai. This sudden shift in curriculum—so
distinct from the previous Ittō-ryū and stage sword techniques (殺陣, Tate) —
bewildered the group's members. Compounding the issue were Ishii Sensei's intense
instruction and perceived favoritism toward the newcomer. Gradually, all the other
members left, and even the junior disciples (otōto-deshi 弟弟子) who had joined
after Tenshin Sensei under Ishii Sensei soon departed as well. As a result, training
continued one-on-one, day after day, from morning until dusk.

Eventually, Ishii Sensei handed over the Kyōdensyo (教伝書*1) to Tenshin Sensei
and granted him Sōden-Inka (相伝印可*2). These instructional texts, printed*3 in the
early Edo period and formatted as bound booklets, were distributed to each
Kumigashira (組頭) for teaching purposes and consisted of four volumes preserved
by the Ishii family. One of these volumes contained a roster of Kōgan samurai, which
connected to the descendants of other Kōgan families. Out of concern that disclosing
such names might cause trouble for those families, Ishii Sensei did not pass on this
particular volume to Tenshin Sensei. These booklets were originally meant to be
destroyed at the time of Kōgan's dissolution to maintain secrecy, which makes their
survival extremely valuable.
Training continued, and in 1965 (Showa 40), Ishii Sensei established the Koken
Hozon Fukyūkai (古剣保存普及会*4). According to Tenshin Sensei, the
organization was formed in part to gain political support from local legislators. In
practice, however, even after Ishii Sensei's passing, Tenshin Sensei—who lacked
social experience and was still learning about human nature—believed he needed the
support of others to preserve and continue the style.

*1 Kyōdensyo 教伝書
Although it is often referred to as Densho (伝書) in other martial traditions, within Tenshinryu it is
specifically called Kyōdensyo (教伝書). Unlike the standard Densho, which typically serves as a
certificate of transmission or succession, the Kyōdensyo functions as a practical instructional text,
distributed to Kumigashira (組頭, group leaders) for the purpose of teaching and training the
samurai under their command.

*2 Sōden Inka 相伝印可

Also known as Kaiden (皆伝), Sōden (相伝) means “ full transmission,” and Inka (印可) means “
formal authorization.” Together, the term signifies that all teachings of the style have been
transmitted and that the recipient is officially recognized as the next Shike (師家, head of the
tradition).

In the brochure and membership application form of the Koken Hozon Fukyūkai, the
origins of Tenshinryu were completely omitted or altered, likely because its history
could not be publicly disclosed. Additionally, the name of the style was changed to
Tenshin Ittō-ryū (天心一刀流*1) as a tsūshō (通称, an alternate or cover name).
Shortly after the organization's founding, however, Tenshin Sensei was involved in a
public brawl*2 in a park. This led to the group's dissolution within a month.
Summoned to the Mitaka Police Station, he was ordered by the police chief to
dissolve Tenshinryu and was banned from teaching. The president of the company
where Tenshin Sensei's father worked had also been appointed an executive of the
group, and after the scandal, Tenshin Sensei was scolded and even

Just one year later, Ishii Sensei was diagnosed with rectal cancer. Entrusting the
style's future to Tenshin Sensei, he passed away. During his lifetime, Ishii instructed
that Tenshin Sensei should take the martial name (Bugō 武号*1) "Tenshin" and teach
Tenshinryu under the name "Hyōhōjuku (兵法塾*2)." He also left a written will
stating that Tenshin Sensei must never disclose Ishii Sensei's own background or the
full origins of the style.
Though unthinkable in modern Japan, half a century ago such historical matters still
had real consequences. Tenshinryu and Kōgan had served the Tokugawa shogunate,
gaining the resentment of many due to their roles—including the dismantling of entire
domains. As vassals of the old regime, they were also treated as enemies of the
imperial court following the Meiji Restoration. If the full history were revealed, it
could have provoked hostility toward both the descendants and the traditions
themselves.
Tenshin Sensei has vowed to uphold Ishii Sensei's wishes throughout his life.

*1 Bugō 武号
In traditional Japanese culture, a Gō (号) is an art name or pseudonym adopted by practitioners in
fields such as the arts, literature, or traditional disciplines like martial arts and tea ceremony. When

used in the context of martial arts (Bujutsu), it is referred to as Bugō (武号). The purpose of a Bugō
is to express one's martial identity, lineage, or philosophy. It is unclear when the term and concept of
Bugō began to be used, or whether it was already in use during the Edo period. *2 Hyōhōjuku 兵法

塾
In the context of Japanese martial arts, Hyōhō (兵法, also read as Heihō) signifies not only
techniques of combat but also a comprehensive system encompassing strategy, tactics, mental

discipline, and personal cultivation. The term Juku (塾) refers to private academies, which became
prominent in the Edo period as institutions where scholars, samurai, and merchants provided
specialized instruction in reading, writing, mathematics, and classical Chinese studies. The term
Juku is still commonly used in Japan today. It refers to private preparatory schools that provide
supplementary education outside of the formal school system, primarily aimed at improving
academic performance and preparing students for school entrance examinations.

Both Kōgan and Tenshinryu were secret traditions. As discussed earlier, even during
the Edo period, the origins of Tenshinryu were deliberately concealed. Additionally, in
the third year of Meireki (1657), Edo Castle was destroyed in the Great Fire of
Meireki ( 明暦の大火 ), also known as the Furisode Fire( 振袖火事 ), except for the
Nishinomaru ( 西の丸 , Western Bailey). The main keep ( 天守 Tenshu or 天守閣
Tenshukaku *16*1) was lost and has never been rebuilt. Though not widely known,
many shogunate records were lost in this fire. If any documents relating to Kōgan had
existed, they were most likely destroyed at that time.

*1 Tenshu 天守, Tenshukaku 天守閣 Tenshu (天守), also called Tenshukaku (天守閣), refers to the
central keep or main tower of a Japanese castle. These tall structures served as both observation
towers and symbols of authority. Originating in the Sengoku period, they continued to be
constructed through the Edo period and were occasionally used for ceremonial purposes or as
storage. As of today, twelve original Tenshu survive. Among them, the keeps of Matsumoto Castle,
Inuyama Castle, Hikone Castle, Himeji Castle, and Matsue Castle have been designated as National
Treasures of Japan. Contrary to common misconceptions, with few exceptions, Tenshukaku were not
used as residences. The living quarters for the lord and his family were typically located in
single-story buildings known as Goten (御殿), which served as their official residential spaces.
