Key Learning Points
— Learn Jo-Ha-Kyū
— Learn breathing
— Learn drawing in Taimen

Points of Caution
— Draw very slowly
It is essential to draw as slowly as possible.
However, if you always do it slowly, the number of repetitions will be insufficient.
It is good to repeat training while keeping a certain speed, and periodically train
with the speed thoroughly reduced.
— About the posture of the first cut (shodachi)
In the first cut, keep the upper body almost vertical in Taimen. Take care not to
take Hanmi or lean forward.
— Use the voice
When striking the Tsukagashira against the Suigetsu, and when thrusting, control
the breath so strongly that the voice comes out. Take care not to do it silently.
— About angles
Be carefully aware of your position, the Taisha's position, the direction you face,
etc., and perform the technique correctly, following the model. If the positions, po‐
sitional relationship or directions are wrong, it becomes a completely different
technique.
— About the gesture of wiping the kissaki
The gesture of wiping the chiabura from the blade is done from around the
monouchi (
: there are various views, but it is said to be about seven-tenths of
the way along the blade) to the kissaki. Only the part to which blood and fat have

not to wipe the part closer to the tsuba than the monouchi.

In this way, in Tenshinryu, the same technique or group of techniques is sometimes
handed down under several names. Each name carries a different meaning, such as
a characteristic or a teaching of the technique.
I cannot speak for other styles, but expressing the same thing with several names is
widely seen in Japanese culture. In ancient times, too, in the *Kojiki* and the
*Nihon Shoki*, the same deity is recorded under several names. This is thought to
be because different names were given according to differences in regional tradi‐
tions, or according to the nature, role and workings of the deity.
Tenshinryu places importance on the meanings held by these multiple names and
on the role played by the words themselves, and hands them down as they are. You
may think it is hard to remember them, but I do not think it is necessary to memo‐
rize them completely.
By encountering the names and their meanings from time to time, your move‐
ments will naturally become refined. The wisdom of those who came before is con‐
tained in this handing down of multiple names.
