About Zahō（座法）
Zahō（座法） refers to the method of sitting—that is, the posture or manner in
which one sits.
It can be a bit confusing, as techniques performed while seated are also
called Zahō（坐法）, but in T enshinryu, the kanji characters for "座" and "
坐" are intentionally distinguished.



Specifically, the character 座 is used when referring to the act or method of
sitting down, while 坐 is used when referring to techniques performed from
a seated position.
In this section, we will introduce the forms of Zahō used in Tenshinryu.

Ōgiza 扇坐
Ōgiza (扇坐) is the most frequently used basic
sitting posture in T enshinryu. It is a kneeling
position in which both knees are folded on the
floor.
T he space between the knees is opened to the
width of approximately two to three fists
placed horizontally.
As a general rule, to ensure the ability to
move freely in case of emergency, the tops of
the feet (toes) should not be placed one over
the other.
However, when sitting for long periods, to
prevent numbness in the legs, one foot may
be intentionally placed on top of the other,
and the position can be adjusted according to
the situation.
Additionally, when preparing to carry out
jōi-uchi (上意討ち a killing carried out on a
lord's orders), as part of prior positioning, the
right foot is placed on top when the technique
involves stepping out with the right foot, and
the left foot is placed on top when the
technique involves stepping out with the left

Fuza 趺坐.

Fuza (趺坐) is generally used as a broad term referring to seated postures in
which the legs are crossed.
However, in T enshinryu, this term specifically refers to Hanza (半坐).
T he character “趺” carries the meaning of sitting cross-legged (agura), but
it also refers to the instep (Kō 甲, top of the foot).
It is thought that the term Fuza originated from the act of placing the instep
of one foot beneath the buttocks, hence the name.

Yagura 矢倉・櫓
Yagura (矢倉 T urret or 櫓 Watchtower), refers
to a seated posture particularly used in Koshiki
古式 forms.
It is especially employed when waiting in
outdoor or battlefield settings.
In this posture, both knees are placed on the
ground with the toes raised. While it closely
resembles Kiza, the knees are opened outward.
T he name comes from likening the position of
the legs to the shape of a yagura or turret
structure.
T he term “ Yagura ” (櫓) originates from
Chinese and originally referred to a watchtower
or elevated platform.
Later in Japan, it came to refer to heiko (兵庫)
— storehouses within castles for storing weapons
— and the term “ yagura ” came to be written
using the characters for “ arrow (Ya 矢)
storehouse(Kura 倉)”, referring to structures
used to store arrows.

Sonkyo 蹲踞
In T enshinryu, Sonkyo 蹲踞 is used in Kumidachi 組太刀 (paired sword
kata), in etiquette involving Nagamono 長物 (long weapons), and in Koshiki
古式 (classical) forms.
It is a seated posture employed when waiting on the battlefield or in outdoor
settings.
T he posture involves lowering the hips into a squat, raising the heels,
placing the buttocks over the heels, and opening the knees outward.
In general usage, a different form of Sonkyo also exists—where both knees are
kept together in a semi-crouched posture.
T his form is particularly used as a waiting position by attendants such as
Chūgen 中間.
Chūgen were household retainers in samurai families who performed miscellaneous
duties for their masters.
