Kaisho, Gyōsho, Sōsho
T hese terms come from calligraphy.
Kaisho 楷書 is the regular script in
which each dot and stroke is written
clearly and precisely. It is also called
shinsho 真書, which is the origin of
the expression shin-gyō-sō 真行草.
Gyōsho 行 書 is the semi-cursive
script, intermediate between kaisho
and sōsho 草書, the cursive script.
Gyōsho relaxes the forms of kaisho,



while sōsho 草書 is still more abbreviated and flowing.
T his sequence does not correspond to the historical order in which the
scripts developed. In learning calligraphy, however, they are generally treated
as three stages: first kaisho, then gyōsho, and finally sōsho.
In T enshinryu, kaisho refers to the fundamental version of a technique
taught to beginners. T his may involve clearly separating the movements or
using a simplified version suitable for initial learning.
T he same technique is then practised in progressively more combat-oriented
forms, classified as gyōsho and sōsho.
[6] T ranslator's note: T he calligraphy analogy distinguishes how a technique
is expressed. In kaisho, its component movements are made clear; in gyōsho
and sōsho, they become increasingly connected and adaptable. These terms do
not refer merely to performing the same movements at different speeds.

Shu-Ha-Ri
Shu-ha-ri 守破離 is said to originate with Sen no Rikyū 千利休 and chanoyu
茶の湯 (Sadō 茶道), the Japanese way of tea. It subsequently came to be
used in many other traditional arts.
At the shu 守 stage, the first priority is to follow the teaching faithfully.
At the ha 破 stage, one's individual characteristics, ideas, and originality
begin to take an appropriate and effective form. D eparting from the
established form becomes acceptable.
At the ri 離 stage, even when acting freely according to one's own
judgement, one no longer departs from the underlying principles. It is
precisely this that makes it possible to move freely beyond the explicit
teaching.
From around the middle of the Edo period, shu-ha-ri is said to have become
widely understood in the way of tea as a progression over a long period of
training.



However, the concept is also said to derive from a verse attributed to Sen no
Rikyū:
「規矩作法守り尽くして破るとも離るるとても本を忘るな」

Kiku sahō mamoritsukushite yaburu tomo hanaruru totemo hon wo wasuruna
> Follow the rules and forms completely.
> Even if you break them or leave them behind,
> never forget their foundation.
T his has also been interpreted as guidance for the everyday practice of tea,
rather than as a description of stages to be reached over many years.
T he point applies to every traditional art. Neither the way of tea nor martial
arts is something practised only for use in the distant future.
In tea, the teaching guides you when receiving guests or attending a tea
gathering. In martial arts, it prepares you for a confrontation that may occur
today, tomorrow, or at any unforeseen moment.
In this context, the meaning is approximately:
> Learn the fundamentals by following them thoroughly. D epending on the
situation, you may need to break from them or move beyond their prescribed
form. Even then, never forget the foundation from which they arise.
T enshinryu also values sustained training over many years. Even more
important, however, are practical effectiveness and adaptability in a
confrontation that may be immediately at hand.
T he precise definition of shu-ha-ri within T enshinryu is not entirely clear to
me. T his may well be because I have not yet fully organised T enshin Sensei's
written notes.
In one sense, it can be understood as a broader progression encompassing
kaisho, gyōsho, and sōsho. For convenience, we also use it to describe stages
of training.
