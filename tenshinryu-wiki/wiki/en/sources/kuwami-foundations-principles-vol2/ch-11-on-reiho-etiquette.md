On Reihō (Etiquette)
Within T enshinryu there is a teaching attributed to Munenori: 'Within the
Nine Rites lies the secret principle. (Kurei ni gokui ari 九礼に極意あり)' T his
is the same as what is called Kuhon-rei 九品礼*1 (the Nine Grades of
Etiquette) in the O gasawara-ryu 小笠原流*2 etiquette tradition.
Reihō is, in essence, the very core of hyoho: by paying respect to the other party,
promoting harmony, and preventing conflict before it arises. At the same time,
it is important as a means to cultivate mind and body through correct
movements. Moreover, many techniques presuppose the etiquette forms of
earlier times; when one learns and masters these properly and is deeply
touched by their spirit, one's understanding of the techniques naturally
deepens. Never treat etiquette lightly.

Shin, Gyō, and Sō
Bows—both standing (Ritsurei 立礼) and seated (Zarei 坐礼)—are broadly
classified into three types according to the angle: Shin 信 (真), Gyō 行, and Sō
草.
Additionally, in any form of etiquette there are cases where one remains in
that posture to wait or to listen attentively.

1, Shin no Rei (Bow)
T he Shin 信 (真) no Rei bow signifies prostration (heifuku 平伏) or an act
of worship (hai 拝).
It is the fundamental salutation when addressing a person of high rank.
*3
With the feeling of the wisteria blossom , lower the head deeply.

*1 It refers to the following nine forms: Mokurei 目礼, Shurei 首礼, Shikenrei 指建礼,
Sōkōrei 爪甲礼, O rite-rei 折手礼, T akushu-rei 拓手礼, Sōshu-rei 雙手礼, Gasshu-rei 合手礼,
and Gasshō-rei 合掌礼.
*2 O gasawara-ryu is a hereditary samurai tradition of etiquette (reihō 礼法), archery, and

those who see it are moved. In other words, it urges us to bow ourselves deeply.



P rostration (heifuku 平伏)

Hanging wisteria blossoms

Lower your head deeply with the image of hanging wisteria blossoms in
mind.*1
Remain in prostration and count to five, then return to upright;
alternatively, perform the bow so that from the start to the return to posture
it takes about three breaths.
When serving as an envoy, or when before the lord, keep the head lowered
until permission is granted; moreover, even when permission is given, do not
raise your face immediately.

2, Gyō no Rei (Bow)
T he Gyō 行 no Rei is the basic form used
when greeting a jōshi 上士 (上司 A
samurai of higher rank than oneself; also a
samurai who is one's direct superior).
With the feeling of ripened rice ears*2,
lower the head.
Keep the head bowed, count to three,
then return to upright;
alternatively, perform the bow so that
from the start through the return it takes
about two breaths.
