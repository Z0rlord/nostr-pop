On Yakujō 約定 (Kinchō 金打)
Yakujō 約定 is a word meaning a promise or agreement. In T enshinryu,
kinchō 金打 is performed as a ceremonial practice at the beginning and end



of training. T his practice is called yakujō.
Kinchō is the act of striking metal objects together to produce a sound, a
practice widely observed during the Edo period. It served as a sign of an
oath, and the word kinchō itself came to mean an oath or promise.
Samurai performed kinchō in various ways: by bringing the tsuba 鍔 (sword
guard) against the koiguchi 鯉口 (mouth of the scabbard), by striking their
*1
sword guards against one another, or by using a kozuka 小柄. Buddhist
monks are said to have used small gongs, while women struck mirrors
together.
T he mysterious resonance of metal was regarded as a sign of purity and
similar qualities. Because metal was precious, the custom also came to
encompass the broader idea of striking objects that were personally valued.
T hese associations helped establish the tradition of kinchō.
In T enshinryu, each training session was preparation for fulfilling one's
responsibilities as a samurai of the Shirin-dan 士林団. T raining itself was
therefore also one of one's official duties.
T raining was undertaken to fulfill this mission. T he practice of yakujō thus
carried various meanings: pledging to devote oneself to training, to value and
thoroughly internalize the teachings received, and not to disclose secret
teachings. With these meanings implicit in the act, it serves to mark the
beginning and end of training.
T he kumigashira 組頭 (group leader) sits in koza 胡坐, while the other
participants sit in ōgiza 扇坐.
First, everyone performs a bow together, using shurei 首礼. T he group
leader then rises from koza to kneel on both knees, while the other
participants raise the right knee (Orishiki 折敷).
