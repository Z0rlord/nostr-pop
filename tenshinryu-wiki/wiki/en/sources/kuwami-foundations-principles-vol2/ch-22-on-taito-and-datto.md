On Taitō 帯刀 and Dattō 脱刀
Taitō 帯刀 means inserting a sword into the sash (kakuobi) at the waist.
Dattō 脱刀 means removing it from the sash (kakuobi). Both actions involve
the sword together with its scabbard; dattō should not be confused with
drawing the blade from the scabbard.
As a rule, a short sword (kodachi 小太刀) is always worn during Tenshinryu
training. The long sword is not normally worn on its own.
T his reflects the principle of training in a manner consistent with the
everyday practices of historical samurai society.
When inserting either sword into the sash (kakuobi) during training or
demonstrations, restrain the tsuba with the thumb. In everyday samurai
practice, however, the scabbard was held slightly beyond the kurikata, toward
the kojiri.
As a rule, put on the wakizashi first, followed by the long sword.
Swords are normally worn with the cutting edge facing upward, although
there are exceptions, as explained below.



When wearing the long sword and preparing to sit in ōgiza, always remove it
before lowering yourself into the seated posture. Do not sit down in ōgiza first
and then remove it.
You may sit in koza, yagura, orishiki, or
sonkyo while still wearing the long sword.
However, in accordance with the
conventions of samurai society*1, you must
avoid sitting in ōgiza while wearing both
swords.
Although this has already been explained,
it requires particular attention. What was
common knowledge in earlier times will
not become habitual for us without
conscious effort. Even someone who has
trained for many years may do this
inadvertently. For those of us living today, learning the everyday conventions
of the past requires firm discipline from the outset.
It cannot be stated that this never occurred during the Edo period.
Nevertheless, it was fundamentally contrary to the conventions of samurai
society. Ishii Sensei regarded it as evidence of a lack of understanding of the
samurai class and Japanese culture, and strictly prohibited it.
O ther schools, however, have practiced or demonstrated techniques while
wearing only the long sword since at least the Edo period.
As explained earlier, the techniques of T enshinryu are organized around the
samurai way of life. Following historical samurai customs and conventions
therefore has particular significance within our school.
D o not use this teaching merely as grounds for criticizing other schools. O ur
responsibility is to preserve T enshinryu. D isparaging or showing contempt
for other traditions has nothing to do with that purpose. Rejecting what
other schools preserve does not demonstrate the superiority or authenticity
of our own tradition.

Sword-Wearing by a Beginner

Beginners tend to hold the sword too vertically. T his makes it harder to
insert and also makes an immediate response more difficult.
Keep looking forward rather than down at the sword or sash (kakuobi).
As in sagetō, position the right thumb on the inner side, avoiding a position
directly over the cutting edge.

*1 T he kakuobi is wrapped around the waist in overlapping layers. T he “ first,” “ second,”
and “ third ” wraps refer to these layers. T he scabbards are inserted between them.



If you lose track of where to insert the sword, briefly look down to locate the
correct position. Hold that opening with the left hand, return your gaze to
the front, and then insert the sword.
Unlike people today, who generally put on and remove swords only during
training and demonstrations, samurai did so frequently in daily life. T hese
principles were intended to prevent such movements from creating openings.

If you have difficulty wearing the sword correctly, do not rush. First, visually check the
position where the sword should be inserted, then use your left hand to create an opening
in the obi (sash). After that, turn to face forward again and insert the sword into the obi.

In the generally used form, the pommel points directly forward. In
T enshinryu, however, particular care is taken to position the guard at the
center of the body.
Viewed from the side, the long and short swords cross to form an X.
T his is the basic method in T enshinryu and is the one best suited to the
school's principles of sword drawing.



2. Sekirei-zashi 鶺鴒指
In sekirei-zashi 鶺鴒指, the end of the
scabbard is lowered slightly.
The name comes from the angle of the
scabbard, which resembles the tail of a
sekirei 鶺鴒, a wagtail.

sekirei 鶺鴒 (a wagtail)

As with kannuki-zashi, the generally used form has the pommel pointing
approximately forward. In T enshinryu, however, the pommel points
diagonally to the right and is positioned at the center of the body.
T he generally used form of sekirei-zashi is said to have been popular during
the Edo period because of its visually pleasing balance.



3. Otoshi-zashi 落指
In otoshi-zashi 落指, the sword is positioned
more upright at the left hip, with the kojiri
lowered and the pommel pointing upward.
T he name refers literally to lowering the end
of the scabbard: otosu 落とす means “ to
lower ” or “ to drop.”
T his method is used in crowds or confined
spaces where the end of the scabbard might
strike someone, when running to prevent the
sword from swinging about, or when using
long weapons or a kusarigama 鎖鎌, a sickle
with an attached chain and weight.

4. Tenjin-zashi 天神指
T he wakizashi and the long sword, or
uchigatana 打刀, are normally worn with
the cutting edge upward. For certain
reasons, however, they may be worn
with the cutting edge downward.
T his method is called tenjin-zashi
because the sword's two ends curve
upward like the kasagi 笠木, the upper
crossbeam of a torii 鳥 居 shrine
gateway.
T enjin is one of the terms used to refer
to deities of the heavenly realm. It is
thought that the name tenjin-zashi 天神
指 originated from the resemblance of
this sword-wearing position to the



kasagi, the uppermost horizontal beam of a
torii 鳥居. However, there are no definitive
historical records or sources confirming this
origin.
A sword in tachi mountings is normally
worn suspended with the cutting edge
downward. When wearing armor and using
an uchigatana, tenjin-zashi 天神指 may be
adopted in a similar manner.
It may also be used during vigorous movement or on horseback to help
prevent sayabashiri, the blade unintentionally slipping out of the scabbard.

On Dattō 脱刀
Removing a sword from the sash (kakuobi) is called dattō 脱刀.
As a rule, remove the long sword first, followed by the wakizashi.
D uring training and demonstrations, restrain the guard with the thumb when
removing the sword. In everyday samurai practice, however, the scabbard was
held slightly beyond the kurikata, toward the kojiri.
