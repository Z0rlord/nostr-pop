About the Folding Fan
In past times, samurai carried
folding fans(Sensu, Ōgi 扇子) with
t h e m t h r o u g h o u t t h e ye a r ,
regardless of the season, and used
them in a variety of ways.
T hey used fans to cool themselves,
to create a breeze by waving them,
to hide their mouths when speaking,
to give directions, or to serve as a
s u b s t i t u t e f o r a t r a y wh e n
presenting items ( this practice
eventually became a formal part of etiquette). Fans were also used as a
symbolic boundary during greetings.
For these reasons, folding fans were essential items for samurai.
Especially in formal settings, samurai used a fan known as a kawahori-sensu
(蝙蝠扇子 bat-shaped fan), which measured one shaku and two sun
(approximately 36 cm) and was typically a white fan. T his size matched the
standard military fan(Gunsen 軍扇) used in battle, and only those of the
samurai class were permitted to carry such fans, making them a status
symbol as well.
In T enshinryu, many techniques make use of the folding fan, reflecting the
samurai trait of always carrying one.
D ue to this cultural background and tactical perspective, in T enshinryu, fans
are inserted at the right waist *1 not only during performances (embu 演武)
but also during regular training.
T here is no strict rule about the exact timing to insert the fan, but it is
easiest to do so after wearing the wakizashi (short sword), as it helps
determine the proper placement.
Hold the middle part of the fan with the right hand, with the pivot facing
left, and insert it deeply at an angle.

T he fan is inserted between the upper
garment and the kakuobi (角帯 stiff
belt).
If inserted too shallowly, it may fall out,
and if inserted vertically, it can interfere
with movements or techniques.
For performances, the kawahori-sensu is
used, while for training, a mai-sen (舞扇
*1
maiōgi, dance fan ) typically used in
Japanese traditional dance is used as a

Even in historical times, samurai did not
always use the kawahori-sensu in daily
life. Instead, they often carried slightly
smaller fans for everyday use.
T enshinryu also includes a simplified
training method called Sensu-Geiko (扇子
稽古 fan training).
In this practice, a folding fan is used as a
substitute for a sword, and partners
practice striking and receiving while
learning techniques.
By carrying a fan regularly and reviewing techniques alone, one can
accelerate their progress in training.

On Jisen, Chisen, Kaisen, and Hasen
In general terminology, holding a
folding fan (sensu 扇子) is called jisen
持扇; placing it down is called chisen
置扇; putting it away inside the front
of the kimono is called kaisen 懐扇;
and picking it up is called hasen 把扇.
After taking your seat, remove the fan
from your waist and hold it in your
left hand, except in formal ceremonial

chisen 置扇

settings (jisen 持扇). In a ceremonial
setting, hold the fan with both hands,
resting it on your thighs (jisen 持扇).
When you need to free your hands,
place the fan beside your right knee
(chisen 置扇), put it away inside the
front of your kimono (kaisen 懐扇),
or tuck it into the sash (kakuobi) at
your waist.
