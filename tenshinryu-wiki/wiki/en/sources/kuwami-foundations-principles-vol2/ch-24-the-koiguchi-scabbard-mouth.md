The Koiguchi (Scabbard Mouth)
T he opening of the scabbard is called the
koiguchi 鯉口, also pronounced koikuchi. T his
name literally means “ carp's mouth ” and comes
from its resemblance to the mouth of a carp (Koi
鯉). It is also known as the sayaguchi 鞘口.
When the sword is sheathed, the blade is
generally suspended within the scabbard without
touching its inner surfaces. T his protects the
surface of the blade from scratches and other
damage caused by rubbing against the scabbard.

Koiguchi 鯉口

T he habaki 鎺・鈨 (blade collar) fits tightly into
the koiguchi, preventing the blade from slipping
easily out of the scabbard. T his condition is
described as “ Koiguchi ga Katai 鯉口が硬い
(a tight koiguchi).”

However, repeatedly drawing and sheathing

the risk of the sword slippin g out
unintentionally. T his condition is described
as “ Koiguchi ga Yurui 鯉口が緩い(a loose

A sword worn by a samurai in everyday life is referred to as sashiryō 差料,
sashimae 差前, or haitō 佩刀. Unlike swords used for practice, these swords
have a tight koiguchi to prevent them from slipping out of their scabbards.
Simply taking hold of the hilt and attempting to draw does not readily
release the blade.

P osition the thumb slightly toward the inside, avoiding the area directly

The farther the hand is from the tsuba, the less force the thumb can exert

index-finger side to touch the tsuba.

Uchi-giri / Kakushi-giri — The Inner or Concealed Release
Uchi-giri 内切り (inner release) is used when initiating a surprise attack,
particularly in zahō 坐法 (techniques performed from a seated position) and
when drawing the short sword.
As its name suggests, the thumb pushes against the tsuba from the inside to
release the sword from the koiguchi. Because the action is concealed behind
the tsuba, it is also called kakushi-giri 隠切り (concealed release).

Since releasing the sword from the koiguchi is an essential preliminary to
drawing, samurai were extremely sensitive to the sight of a thumb being
placed against the tsuba. Uchi-giri can therefore delay an opponent's reaction
slightly.
In seated techniques, the hands are particularly easy to see, making this
method important when taking an opponent by surprise.
There is also a form of Uchi-giri in which, instead of pushing with the thumb
alone, you close the hand into a fist and press against the tsuba with the
thumb and index finger together.
T his requires gripping even closer to the koiguchi than usual, close enough
for the hand to touch the tsuba. It is an effective method when the koiguchi
is particularly tight.
When taking hold of the scabbard with the left hand in a reverse grip, the
little-finger side of the hand may also be used to perform the release.

T he method is called Hikae-giri because the index finger also serves to

T he position in which these two fingers restrain the tsuba after the koiguchi
has been released is called Hikae-tsuba 控え鍔. Hikae-tsuba is also used
during nōtō 納刀 (sheathing the sword) and in other situations.
Hikae-giri is generally not used when drawing suddenly. Instead, it is used
when facing an opponent or in similar circumstances where the koiguchi can
be released in advance, leaving the sword ready to draw at any moment.
Restraining the tsuba helps prevent the sword from slipping out because of
an unexpected movement or jolt. It also helps prevent another person from
pulling the sword out of the scabbard.

Precautions Concerning the Fingers
T hree methods of releasing the sword from the koiguchi have been
introduced above.
Whichever method is used, one point requires particular care: as you move
from releasing the koiguchi to drawing the sword, bend the thumb and, if



used, the index finger, and place them against the scabbard.
If these fingers remain extended, there is a risk of cutting them should the
direction of the sword tip change unexpectedly. For example, the hilt may
strike an opponent's blade, or a strong wind or another unforeseen
occurrence may alter the sword's movement.
Ishii Sensei referred to drawing with the fingers left extended after releasing
the koiguchi as “ chī-chī-pappa ちーちーぱっぱ” and strictly warned against
this dangerous habit.

Maintaining the Koiguchi
As explained above, the swords that samurai wore in everyday life differed



from those used for training.
In earlier times, training swords often had simple koshirae 拵え (sword
mountings), and their maintenance was not always carried out with the same
meticulous care.
P arts whose failure can directly cause an accident, such as the mekugi 目釘
(the peg securing the hilt to the blade's tang), require frequent attention.
However, vigorous drawing practice can make frequent repairs to the
koiguchi necessary. Maintaining it as meticulously as that of an everyday
sword could result in spending more time on repairs than on training.
For this reason, training swords often had loose koiguchi. T he custom of
restraining the tsuba during Sagetō 提刀 (carrying the sheathed sword at
one's side in the hand) developed as a way of preventing the sword from
slipping out(sayabashiru 鞘走る). T his practice is common to many classical
Styles, including T enshinryu.
At the same time, an excessively worn koiguchi can interfere with training.
Leaving it unattended for too long may also cause it to break. P eriodic repair
is therefore necessary.
T he basic repair method is to attach a thin piece
of kyōgi 経木 to the inside of the scabbard
opening.
Kyōgi is a very thin sheet of wood.
Cut the kyōgi to a suitable size, apply adhesive,
and attach it to the worn area. In earlier times,
rice paste was used as the adhesive. T oday, wood
glue can be used; for training swords, a
quick-drying type is recommended.
