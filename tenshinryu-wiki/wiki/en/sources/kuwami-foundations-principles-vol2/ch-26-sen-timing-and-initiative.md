Sen — Timing and Initiative
Sen 先 is a classification of timing in attack and defence.
Each kata 勢法 is based on a particular relationship of timing and initiative.
Unless you understand the sen assumed by the technique you are practising,
your training will not be correct. Rather than improving, you may actually
become less skilful.
Fundamentally, samurai were warriors. T hey valued the mindset of
jōzai-senjō 常在戦場, remaining prepared as though always on the battlefield
and never forgetting the possibility of combat, even in everyday life.
According to this ideal, therefore, even an unexpected attack should not
truly catch a samurai unprepared.

Interestingly, warrior culture also recognised conventions for surprise attacks
(Fui-uchi 不意打ち). T hese included calling out before cutting at someone
from behind, or waking a sleeping opponent before killing them.
T he underlying assumption was that a warrior should already be mentally
prepared, so an unexpected attack was not necessarily considered cowardly.
Calling out nevertheless showed respect for the opponent and, in that sense,
prevented the attack from being a complete surprise.
O f course, this does not mean that every samurai observed these
conventions.
P erhaps reflecting this cultural background, martial traditions make relatively
little use of the concept of gote 後手, being forced into a belated response.
A martial practitioner is expected to remain vigilant. T o fall behind the
opponent is therefore understood as suffering the consequences of one's own
carelessness. In this reasoning, the fault lies in one's own lack of
preparedness rather than in the opponent's supposed cowardice.
For this reason, the concept of gote is generally avoided. Even when the
opponent attacks first, the response is described as go-no-sen 後の先, rather
than gote.
T hese concepts are also important to understanding the complexities of
warrior culture.
T he following explains the names and types of sen used in T enshinryu.

Sen-sen-no-sen — Acting Before the Attack Begins
Sen-sen-no-sen 先々の先 means perceiving the opponent's intention to attack
and the very inception of that attack, then acting to control it.
You anticipate the attack and move before the opponent physically begins to
act.

It refers to an offensive sword action in which you initiate the attack.
T his corresponds to taking the first move in the ordinary sense.

Sen-no-go — Controlling the Response You Provoke
Sen-no-go 先の後 means initiating an attack to make the opponent react,
then controlling the movement they make in response.

Ki-no-sen — Seizing the Initiative Through Pressure
Ki-no-sen 気の先 is the principle of using kiate 気当て to seize the
initiative.
Kiate is a collective term for using body positioning and movement, the eyes,
forceful presence, and kiai 気合.
Examples include making a large threatening movement, glaring, projecting
determination, giving a kiai, or stamping the feet to make the opponent
hesitate or recoil.

Tai-no-sen — Attacking Almost Simultaneously
Tai-no-sen 対の先, also called taitai-no-sen 体々の先, means attacking at
almost the same moment as the opponent, in a manner comparable to a
counter.
T his timing is more commonly used in unarmed combat and is not
frequently employed in swordsmanship.

Go-no-sen — Responding to the Initial Attack
Go-no-sen 後の先 means dealing with an opponent's initial attack and then
counterattacking.
Because it involves an actual exchange of attack and defence, it appears in
many techniques.

Go-no-go — Responding After a Series of Attacks
Go-no-go 後の後 means dealing with a series of approximately three sword
attacks and then counterattacking when the opponent has exhausted that
sequence of attacks.
