Kata — Structured Training
In period dramas, films, manga, and other works of fiction, martial training
is usually depicted as sparring (Shiai 仕合).
Characters strike one another with Bokken 木剣 (wooden swords), and
sometimes even exchange forceful blows with live blades. Yet a blow from a
Bokken 木剣 can break bones or, if it lands in a vulnerable place, cause
immediate death. Forceful clashes between live blades can quickly render the
swords unusable.
Such scenes also commonly show characters using their everyday swords
rather than weapons intended for training. From a practical standpoint,
these depictions are implausible.
Advanced practitioners can conduct controlled sparring even with Bokken 木
剣 or live blades by using sundome 寸止め*1, stopping their attacks just short
of contact. Such practice can indeed be useful.
However, because this lacks the dramatic impact sought in entertainment,
unrealistic training scenes have repeatedly been portrayed. T he serious
misunderstandings about martial arts that result are a matter of concern.
In actual martial training, the central practice is learning the techniques and
*2
tactics preserved in kata developed by the founder, successive shike 師家
(heads of the Style), and shihan 師範 (senior instructors).
These are learned through yakusoku-geiko 約束稽古(Yakusoku means
"promise" in Japanese.), practice in which the actions are agreed in advance.
This is called kata-geiko 型稽古(形稽古).
The word kata is usually written 型 or 形. Tenshinryu, however, writes it 勢
法.

*1 Sun (寸) is a traditional unit of length formerly used in Japan. O ne sun (寸) is
equivalent to approximately 3.03 cm.
*2 Although many Styles have used the title sōke 宗家 since the Meiji period,



We avoid 型*1, the character also used for a mould, because the teaching is
not to become trapped in a fixed mould.
*2
We also avoid 形 , which refers to outward shape or appearance, to
emphasize that training is not about merely following prescribed steps, going
through the motions, or simply imitating how a technique looks on the
outside.
T he character sei 勢 expresses force, momentum, and active movement. It
also carries meanings associated with military forces and strength.
T he character hō 法 means a law or principle and, in Buddhism, can also
signify truth.
T he characters are deliberately assigned to the reading kata to express the
essence of T enshinryu: using shin-gi-tai 心技体, mind (心), technique (技),
and body (体), in a manner appropriate to the situation.
Kata-geiko 勢法稽古 is indispensable to learning attack and defence and
occupies a central place in training.
Strictly speaking, however, Tenshinryu reserves the name kata for certain
particularly distinguished elements of its curriculum.
In T enshinryu, other methods are simply referred to by terms such as gihō
技法 (techniques).
Because this distinction can be confusing, kata is sometimes used more
broadly for convenience. In its strict sense, however, the designation
primarily applies to:
- T welve standing sword-drawing kata.
- Seven seated short-sword drawing kata.
- Seven kumidachi 組太刀 (swordsmanship techniques).
Everything else is classified as gihō, although these techniques may be
understood as equivalent to what is generally called kata elsewhere.
For convenience, gihō may also be referred to as kata during instruction
and in other situations.

*1 Kata (型) literally means "mold" or "form," as in a mold used to cast or shape an
object.
*2 Katachi (形) means the outwardly visible shape or appearance of an object.
