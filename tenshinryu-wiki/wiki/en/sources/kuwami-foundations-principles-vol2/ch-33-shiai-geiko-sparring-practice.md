Shiai-geiko — Sparring Practice
Sparring practice, or shiai-geiko 仕合稽古, using marudachi 丸太刀 has
been practised since earlier times, both in T enshinryu and in other Styles.
*1

From the middle of the Edo period onward, the development of protective
equipment and shinai 竹刀*2, which became the basis of modern kendo, made
safer sparring possible.
As a result, training in many Styles came to place excessive emphasis on
sparring, while kata(勢法／形／型) practice received less attention.
T he clear outcome of whether a strike landed, the safety that reduced fear,
and the greater amount of physical activity gave sparring a sporting and
recreational appeal. It spread widely across many Styles and has continued to
the present day.
Even today, the view that kata practice is meaningless and that training
centred on sparring is more rational remains deeply rooted.
Certainly, kata practice that
has become an empty
repetition of outward forms
has lost its effectiveness.
Yet kata developed by earlier
warriors through encounters in
which lives were at stake
cannot simply be dismissed as
meaningless.

*1 Generally known as fukuro-shinai (袋竹刀), this is a training implement used as a
substitute for a sword, made from bamboo split at one end and covered with lacquered
leather. Maru (丸) means "round," and Maru-tachi (丸太刀) therefore literally means "a
sword with a rounded shape."
*2 A bamboo practice sword believed to have been developed from the fukuro-shinai and
introduced into kenjutsu (swordsmanship) training around the middle of the Edo period.
It is still used in modern kendo.



In many cases, this misunderstanding arises from having encountered only
kata practice that has already lost its purpose and substance.
Unrestricted exchanges of attack and defence are indeed an important form
of training. However, excessive attachment to them can produce skills
specialised for particular rules or substitute weapons.
History shows how such training can develop into a sport while losing its
substance as a martial art.
For shiai 仕合 (practice matches) to contribute to the development of
martial skills, the following conditions must be met.
1. Practitioners must first undergo sufficient Kata-geiko (kata training) and
attain a high level of proficiency.
2. Rather than merely swinging lightweight, safe training weapons,
practitioners must use them as substitutes for actual swords, performing only
movements that they would also be capable of executing with a real sword.
3. Shiai must not consist merely of reckless charges or suicidal attacks made
possible by the safety of the training environment.
4. Above all, practitioners must approach shiai with a purpose beyond simply
winning or losing.
Naturally, shiai may sometimes be conducted to demonstrate one's strength.
Nevertheless, they remain substitutes for actual combat and are, ultimately, a
form of training.
O f course, there is also what T enshinryu calls shiai 死合 (life-or-death
combat), which is not training but a genuine fight in which one's life is at
stake.
In such situations, including duels known as hatashiai 果たし合い (duels
fought to settle a challenge), victory is paramount. T he same applies to
certain forms of shiai conducted to determine the superiority of one
practitioner or Style over another, rather than to promote mutual exchange
between different Styles. Such matches are known as taryu-jiai 他流仕合
(matches against other martial arts Styles).



When the superiority and pride of a Style are at stake, winning becomes the
foremost objective, even if wooden swords or other training weapons are
used.
However, in many cases, shiai is conducted as a form of recreation,
stimulating the competitive spirit and allowing participants to enjoy the
excitement of winning and losing.
T his, too, is one of the pleasures of martial arts, and it should not be
dismissed lightly.
Nevertheless, shiai training, which should originally be no more than one
method of becoming stronger, must never become an end in itself. T his is a
pitfall into which many martial arts Styles have fallen throughout history,
and one against which we must be extremely cautious.
It appears that this problem had already been recognized during the time of
Lord Yagyu Munenori.
T he following passages appear in Tsuki no Sho 月之抄, a martial arts treatise
written by his eldest son, Yagyu Jubei.

On the Proper Mindset for Shiai (仕相之心持之事)
O riginal text:
父云、初メ一度二度ヲ専ら心得へし。むかしヨリ、サル事アリ。シナイニス
ミヲぬり、付タル方ヲ勝ト定メ、ウタレテモ、シャウイン（承引）セサリシ
トいへり。キヤウ（器用）ニジヤウツル（乗ずる）トモ、数ヲセザレノイマ
シメナリ。心得ヘキナリ。
English translation:
"In shiai (practice matches), one should generally limit the exchange of
blows to just one or two, rather than engaging in prolonged exchanges.
T here is an old story concerning this. Ink was applied to shinai (bamboo
practice swords), and whoever succeeded in leaving an ink mark on the
opponent was declared the winner. Yet it is said that some refused to
acknowledge defeat even after being struck.



T his serves as a warning against continuing to exchange blows excessively,
even when one possesses considerable skill and is tempted to rely upon it.
T his teaching must be properly understood."
On the Proper Mindset for Training (稽古之心持之事)
O riginal text:
父云、仕合ヲ重（おもんず）へからす。おしへヲまもり、ツゝシミヲ可得心。
（中略）亦、初・中・後之けいこ至り、習ヲ知りテ、シロウト（素人）ゝ仕
合シテ稽古トすへし。初・中・後三段、習ヲ未極シテハ無用也。稽古のびざ
るものナリ。
English translation:
My father said:
"O ne must not place excessive importance on shiai. Follow the teachings and
maintain a disciplined and cautious attitude.
[P assage omitted]
Furthermore, once one has progressed through the initial, intermediate, and
advanced stages of training and has understood the teachings, one may
engage in shiai with inexperienced practitioners as a means of further
training.
However, unless one has thoroughly mastered the teachings of all three
stages, such practice is of no use. It will not lead to improvement in one's
training."

Shiai is an indispensable part of martial arts training. However, the
excitement experienced within its relatively safe environment has a certain
addictive quality.
Amid the desire to win and the excitement of competition, practitioners risk
becoming absorbed in a world far removed from the fundamental purpose of
martial training: preparing for combat in which life and death are at stake.
T he practical methods of shiai training will be taught separately. For now,
however, it is essential to understand the principles explained above.
