Metsuke — The Use of the Eyes
In classical martial arts, the way the eyes are directed and attention is
distributed during training or combat is called metsuke 目付. It is considered
an important part of shinpō 心法.*1
T here is a Japanese expression meaning “ to notice what others do not.”
Much of the information available to us comes through vision.
Humans rely heavily on vision to obtain information from the outside world,
and it is commonly said that visual information accounts for approximately
80% of the total.
In actual combat, however, you cannot consciously identify a particular form
of metsuke, connect it to the opponent's movement, and then decide how to
respond.
What matters is using practice, in which the movements are known in
advance, to connect preparatory signs, the movements that follow them, and
the appropriate responses. In this way, you train yourself to respond
correctly without conscious deliberation.

Hoshi-Mine-Tani-Yama — Fundamental Metsuke
Hoshi 星(Stars)-Mine 峰(P eaks)-Tani 谷(Valleys)-Yama 山(Mountains)
identifies fundamental points to observe when reading an opponent's
movement.

*1 Shinpō is a broad term encompassing mental attitudes and principles in martial practice,
various psychological processes, and the cultivation of the mind.



T hese are the visual checkpoints on which
attention is directed.
In Tenshinryu, the fists are first likened to hoshi
星 (stars).
O ne fist is called issei 一星 (one star), or
hitotsuboshi 一つ星 (one star). Both fists are
called nisei 二星 (two stars), or futatsuboshi 二つ
星 (two stars).
The teaching is to observe these “ stars ” and
anticipate the next movement.
Because movements of the fists are among the
clearest indications of how a sword is being
handled, this can be regarded as the most
important form of metsuke.
Next come mine-tani 峰谷 (peaks and valleys).
These refer to the rising and falling movements of
the upper arms, between the shoulders and elbows.
When the upper arm rises, it prepares for a downward cut. Observe the
rising arm as a mine 峰 (peak) to anticipate the cut.
Conversely, when the upper arm lowers, it prepares for an upward cut.
Observe the lowered arm as a tani 谷 (valley) to anticipate that movement.
Next is yama 山 (mountain).
This is called enzan 遠山 (distant mountain), and likens the two shoulders to
a mountain.
T he teaching is to observe the movement of both shoulders to anticipate
what comes next.
T his is particularly important when the fists cannot be seen clearly, such as
when the opponent takes Suigetsu-no-kurai 水月の位.



Tachiai-no-shinbō — Principles of Perception in an
Encounter
T enshinryu includes various forms of metsuke suited to different
circumstances and different aspects of movement. T he following introduces
several representative teachings under Tachiai-no-shinbō 立相之心法.

Mi-bure — Subtle Signs in the Body
Mi-bure 躰振れ, also written 躰震れ, concerns subtle indications in the body,
particularly the face and shoulders.
Mi 躰 means "body," and Bure 振れ・震れ means "to tremble" or "to
shake."
T his metsuke observes small changes in expression, shoulder movement, and
similar signs that precede an action.

Ji-bure — Signs in the Legs
Ji-bure 地揺れ, also written 地振れ, concerns the action of the legs.
Ji 地 means "ground," and Bure 揺れ・振れ means "shaking."
It is particularly directed toward movements of the knees in seated
techniques.
Unless the distance is already as close as shin-no-ma 心の間, the opponent
must advance one leg or the other to reach you. Without movement of the
knees, they cannot harm you.

Shin-no-me — Perceiving the Mind
Shin-no-me 心の眼 means observing the opponent as though seeing into
their mind.
Shin 心 means "mind," and Me 眼 means "eye."
An intention arises first, and movement, including its preliminary signs,
follows.
T his is a form of attentive perception that recognises what is happening
within the opponent through their words, actions, and other indications.



Shin-no-me — The Discerning Eye
Shin-no-me 真の目 is the precise use of the eyes to read the opponent's
movements.
Shin 真 means "truth," and Me 目 means "eye." T ogether, they refer to
possessing an eye capable of discerning the truth.
T o acquire the perception described in the preceding section, you must first
learn to distinguish movements in detail.

Kasumi-me — The Mist-Like Gaze
Kasumi-me 霞目 means looking as though gazing at distant mist (kasumi
霞), without shifting the eyes about.
D o not open the eyes wide; keep them slightly narrowed.
T he important point is to avoid fixing the gaze rigidly or allowing visual
attention to become stuck.

Kan-no-me — Observing the Whole
Kan-no-me 観の眼 directs attention toward the opponent's entire form.
Kan means "observation," and Me means "eye." T ogether, they refer to the
ability to observe the whole picture.
Information is drawn from the whole rather than from a single isolated
point.

Tōgan / Sukashi-me — Seeing Through
Tōgan 透眼, also called sukashi-me 透目, is the perceptive use of the eyes to
discern such things as what is happening behind the opponent's tsuba and
how their tenouchi is working.
T ō 透 (sukashi) means "to see through," and Gan 眼, (me 目) means "eye."
It means reading through outward appearances to discern the opponent's
intentions and plans.



Yō-no-me and Kage-no-me
— Seeing the Visible and the Concealed
Within kan-no-me, there is also kage-no-me 影の目.
Kage 影 means "shadow," and Me 目 means "eye."
Yō-no-me 陽の目 observes what is visible. Kage-no-me is equally important:
it discerns what is concealed, such as hidden weapons, schemes, deceptive
attacks, and feints.
Yō 陽 means "positive," and Me 目 means "eye." In'yō (陰陽) represents
two opposing principles.
T his teaching is also called yōei-no-koto 陽影の事.
石井先生口伝 Ishii Sensei Kuden
「極意は睫の如くに身近にあり影の目を養え」
Gokui ha Matsuge no gotokuni Mijika ni ari Kage no me wo yashinae

“ The deepest teachings are as close to you as your eyelashes. Cultivate
kage-no-me.”
— An oral teaching of Ishii Sensei
T his principle is also taught as important when receiving instruction.

Sute-me / Nagashi-me — Unobtrusive Observation
Sute-me 捨て目 and nagashi-me 流眄 are teachings on gathering information
by observing the surroundings without making that observation apparent to
others, particularly when checking behind you.
Sute means "to discard," and Me means "eye." Nagashi means "to flow," and
Me means "eye." Nagashime refers to looking at something by moving only
the eyes, without moving the head, to bring it into one's field of vision.
T hey serve many purposes: checking the footing, detecting concealed
opponents, locating allies, and identifying escape routes for yourself or the
opponent.
