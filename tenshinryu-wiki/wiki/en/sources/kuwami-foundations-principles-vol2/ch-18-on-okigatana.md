On Okigatana 置刀
In earlier times, samurai generally removed the long sword from the waist
when entering a building. T hey therefore did not normally wear the long
sword indoors or remain seated with it at the waist.
In many classical martial arts schools, techniques are practiced from seiza 正
座 while wearing only the long sword. T his would have been extremely
unusual in the context of Edo-period samurai etiquette. For this reason,
some people disparagingly refer to such practices as “ seiza iai.”
However, these are training practices, and each school preserves important
teachings with their own significance. We who live in the present day should
certainly not criticize them.
At the same time, a school's teachings are its identity. Just as other schools
value their own traditions, we must place even greater importance on the
traditions of T enshinryu.
T herefore, during Tenshinryu training, never sit down in ōgiza 扇坐 while
wearing both the long and short swords.
T here may be unavoidable exceptions—for example, when teaching without
enough wakizashi 脇指 available, the long sword may be worn as a substitute
for the wakizashi while sitting in ōgiza. Apart from such rare cases, always
remove the long sword before taking your seat.
After sitting down, place the honzashi 本指 (the long sword), beside or
behind you. T his is called okigatana 置刀, also known as yasumedachi 休太
刀.
Okigatana does not simply mean putting a sword down. As a warrior, you
keep it as close as possible so that you are always prepared for combat. It is
not a sword abandoned on the floor.
In practice, however, samurai sometimes moved away from swords they had
set down during daily life. T oday, too, swords may be left on the floor
during training.



P recisely for this reason, okigatana teaches you to remain attentive even to
how the sword is placed, so that you do not fail to take it up in an
emergency or mistakenly pick up someone else's sword. Understand that this
is a matter of practical awareness, rather than merely ceremonial etiquette.
T here are three ways to place the sword: In 陰, Yō 陽, and An 暗. T he
methods and points requiring attention are explained below.

3. An no Okigatana 暗の置刀 — Placing the Sword Behind You
T his means placing the sword behind you, on the An 暗 side, when seated.
P lacing the sword behind your back expresses greater respect than placing it
on the right. It is the most respectful arrangement when you retain the
sword rather than entrusting it to someone else.
It is also used when placing the sword on either side would cause an
obstruction.
Normally, position the pommel toward In 陰, your right, and the tip toward
Yō 陽, your left.
Keep the cutting-edge side facing toward your body, and place the sword
crosswise behind you, parallel to your back. D epending on the technique
being practiced, it may sometimes be placed at a slight angle.



As far as possible, align the midpoint between the pommel and the end of
the scabbard with the center of your body.
T he direction of the tip changes according to the position of the kamiza 上
座, the place of honor.
Especially at ceremonial gatherings or occasions where differences in rank
are clearly defined, if the kamiza is to your right or left, place the sword so
that its tip does not point toward it.
When visiting someone's home, you may entrust your sword to a samurai
household attendant or a member of the household at the entrance. Where a
relationship of trust exists, they may place it behind you after you have taken
your seat.
In such cases, the sword may be placed with the cutting-edge side facing
away from you. You also cannot assume whether the hilt will point to your
right or left.
T o avoid being caught unprepared in an emergency, it is important to
discreetly check beforehand how the sword has been placed.
