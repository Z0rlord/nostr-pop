On Sagetō 提刀
Carrying the long sword in one hand is called sagetō 提刀, also written 下げ
刀. T hree forms are used according to the situation.

1. Ordinary Sagetō

Sagetō used in everyday life

Sagetō used in training and demonstrations

This is the form normally used in everyday circumstances.
Hold the sword by the scabbard (saya 鞘), with the cutting edge facing
upward, the hilt pointing forward, and the tip pointing backward.
D uring training and demonstrations, hold the scabbard near its mouth
(koiguchi 鯉口) with the right hand, and restrain the sword guard (tsuba 鍔)
with the thumb. T his prevents sayabashiru 鞘 走 る —the blade
unintentionally slipping out of the scabbard.
When restraining the guard, place the thumb on the inner side rather than
directly over the cutting edge. T his keeps the thumb out of the blade's path
should the blade slip out.
In the everyday life of a samurai, however, the scabbard was held beyond the
kurikata 栗形, toward the kojiri 小尻, the end of the scabbard. T he kurikata
is the knob through which the scabbard cord (sageo 下緒) passes. Holding
the sword farther down the scabbard showed that one could not readily

2. Ceremonial Sagetō
This form is used on particularly
formal occasions, such as before a
deity or in the presence of a lord.
With the cuttin g edge facing
downward and the tip pointing
forward, hold the scabbard near the
koiguchi with the right hand.
Restrain the guard with the
little-finger side of the palm.

Because the hilt points backward, this
position is unsuitable for an immediate
draw. T he cutting edge is not directed
forward, and the tip is lowered. It is
therefore a respectful posture that
expresses no hostile intent toward the
person in front of you.
In other schools, the scabbard is often
held beyond the kurikata, toward its
end. In T enshinryu, however, the guard
is restrained as a precaution against
someone grasping the hilt from behind.

Sagetō as Practiced in Other Schools



As explained above, the guard was not normally restrained in the everyday
form of sagetō. In ceremonial sagetō, however, restraining the guard does not
enable you to release the blade at the koiguchi. T he guard is therefore
restrained as a rule.

3. Sagetō for a Tachi 太刀
This form is used particularly
when carrying a deeply curved
tachi 太刀*1 in a ceremonial
setting.
T he only difference from the
preceding ceremonial form is
the direction of the cutting
edge. With a deeply curved
tachi, the cutting edge faces
upward.
If the cutting edge faces downward, the deep curvature causes the tip to
point forward. T urning the cutting edge upward prevents this.
P ointing the tip toward another person presents the greatest threat;
directing the cutting edge toward them comes next. Following this order of
priority, the direction of the cutting edge is reversed to avoid pointing the
tip at the other person.
T his arrangement is also said to
help prevent the scabbard from
splitting and the cutting edge
from being chipped if the sword
is kicked while being carried.
When the cutting edge faces downward, the
curvature of the blade causes the tip to point forward.

*1 A tachi 太刀 is a type of Japanese long sword traditionally worn suspended from the
belt with the cutting edge downward. H ere, the instructions concern carrying a deeply
curved tachi in the hand, rather than wearing it at the waist.
