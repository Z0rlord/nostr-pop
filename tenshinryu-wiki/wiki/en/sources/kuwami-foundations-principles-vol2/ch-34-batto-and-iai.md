Battō and Iai
Iai 居合 is the name said to have been given to a sword-drawing art by
Hayashizaki Jinsuke 林崎甚助, a master swordsman of the Sengoku *1 and
Azuchi-Momoyama periods*2.
O riginal note: Hayashizaki Jinsuke is said to have been born in 1542, the
eleventh year of the T enbun era, in what is now Yamagata P refecture. His
father was killed in an ambush during his childhood, and he devoted himself
to martial training with the intention of avenging him.
In 1559, the second year of the Eiroku era, he undertook a period of
devotional retreat at Hayashizaki Myōjin Shrine. Through dedicated practice, he
is said to have attained a profound insight into the art of iai.
After avenging his father, he taught the art he had developed to his disciples.
He consequently came to be regarded as the founder, or alternatively the
reviver, of iaijutsu.
T he year of his death is unknown, but he is believed to have died in the
early Edo period. A monument at Renkeiji T emple in Kawagoe
commemorates the site as the place where he spent his final days.
T he term later came to be used more broadly for sword drawing, and Styles
unrelated to Hayashizaki Jinsuke's line of transmission also adopted it.
Iai and battō 抜刀 consequently came to be widely understood as
synonymous. T enshinryu, however, does not use iai in this sense.

*1 T he Sengoku period (Warring States period) generally refers to approximately 100
years from the late 15th century to the late 16th century. It is commonly considered to
have begun in 1467 with the outbreak of the Ōnin War and to have ended either in 1568,
when O da Nobunaga entered Kyoto, or in 1573, when the Muromachi shogunate came to an
end. However, there are several interpretations regarding when the period ended.
*2 T he Azuchi-Momoyama period generally refers to approximately 30 years, from 1568,
when O da Nobunaga entered Kyoto, to 1600, when the Battle of Sekigahara took place.
H owever, there are several interpretations regarding when the period ended.



Among the early members of the Shirin-dan 士林団 were masters of various
martial arts Styles. According to tradition, some of them were accomplished
practitioners of Tamiya-ryu 田宮流, a Style whose lineage traces back to
Hayashizaki Jinsuke. It is also said that these practitioners influenced the
sword-drawing techniques of T enshinryu.
However, the sword-drawing techniques of T enshinryu were also developed
upon the foundation of techniques passed down within the Yagyu clan 柳生
家. T hus, although influenced by T amiya-ryu, T enshinryu's sword-drawing
techniques developed into a distinct system of their own.
In T enshinryu, drawing the sword is referred to as bakken 抜剣, battō 抜刀,
battōjutsu 抜刀術, or simply nuki 抜き.
T enshinryu sometimes uses the term iai 居相 with a different meaning, but
this does not refer to drawing the sword.
T enshinryu has refrained from using iai as a name for its sword-drawing art
because distinctions between lines of transmission are important.
O riginally, the name iai embodied the particular character of the Styles that
used it. Adopting it casually merely because its general meaning appears
similar could risk eroding T enshinryu's distinctiveness and identity.
T hings that appear alike may nevertheless differ in ways that determine the
outcome of a life-or-death encounter.
T o preserve its transmission as fully as possible for future generations,
T enshinryu therefore exercises great care even in its choice of terminology.
