On Ken-zazen 剣坐禅
At Kōgan 光願, daily training began
with basic exercises and other activities
as a warm-up. Afterward, the low
windows (jimado 地窓*1) in the training
hall were opened, and practitioners sat
in a row before them, or along the
veran da, to perform zazen while
receiving the morning sunlight.

jimado 地窓

T his was an important ritual for
clearing away everyday distractions
and focusing on training. It also
served to cultivate fudōshin 不動心,
an unwavering mind, by maintaining
the awareness of always being on the
battlefield (jōzai-senjō 常在戦場) and
remaining conscious of death.

kekkafuza 結跏趺坐

In Zen Buddhism, sitting postures such as kekkafuza 結跏趺坐 (the
full-lotus posture)
are commonly used. In T enshinryu, however,
practitioners sit in koza 胡坐.
T his meditation is performed as part of training, either with the swords worn
at the waist or with both swords placed nearby. It is called ken-zazen 剣坐禅,
“ sword zazen,” to express the idea of never being separated from the sword.

T here are three ways to arrange the swords:
Keep both the long and short swords at the waist.
Remove only the long sword and place it to the right, left, or rear: In 陰, Yō
陽, or An 暗.
Remove both swords. P lace the long sword to the right, left, or rear, and
place the short sword crosswise beneath both thighs.
First, join the palms together in front of the chest. T hen bring the hands
together with the left hand underneath and the right hand on top.
P lace the hands against the lower abdomen at the seika tanden 臍下丹田 .
Leave a gap approximately the thickness of a sheet of paper between the
thumbs, taking care not to let them touch.
