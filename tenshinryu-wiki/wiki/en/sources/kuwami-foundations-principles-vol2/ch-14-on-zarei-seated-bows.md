On Zarei (Seated Bows)
Zarei 坐礼 (seated bows) is the etiquette form used indoors.
Within T enshinryu there are various seated bows appropriate to different
situations.
Learn the correct forms so you can apply them flexibly as needed.

1, Me-otoshi 目落 — Sō 草 no Rei
Also called mokurei 目礼. Mokurei literally means “ bow(rei 礼) of the eyes
(moku 目)” in Japanese.
It is an eshaku 会釈, an abbreviated bow.
Meotoshi 目落 literally means 'to lower(otoshi 落) the eyes(me 目)' in
Japanese, but in practice you do not move the eyeballs; instead, you slightly
incline the upper body.
T his naturally lowers the line of sight, hence the name me-otoshi.
D o not tilt only the neck, as that rounds the back.
It is also used when returning a bow or giving a reply.

2, Shurei 首礼 — Sō 草 no Rei
Shurei 首礼 is performed by leaning the
body slightly deeper than the me-otoshi 目
落 bow.
It is an eshaku 会釈, an abbreviated bow.
D o not bend only the neck; instead,
incline the upper body so that the head
tilts naturally.
If you tilt only the neck, the back rounds
and the bow appears ungainly.
Shurei 首礼 is also used when returning a
bow or giving a reply.

3, Shikenrei 指建礼 — Gyō 行 no Rei
Shikenrei 指建礼 is a bow performed
from Ōgi-za 扇坐, placing the fingertips
of both hands on the floor.
T his abbreviated bow is used frequently
in training and in demonstrations.
Because the fingers are set upright when
bowing, it is called shikenrei 指建礼 (It

literally means “ finger-raised bow ” in
Japanese — shi 指 ‘ finger ’, ken 建 ‘ to erect/ raise ’, rei 礼 ‘ bow ’.).
Keep the hands open, folding the thumbs slightly inward so the thumbnails
are not visible.
T here are two variants. T he common one places the extended fingertips
beside the knees.
However, if a sword is set to the side, using that variant can be mistaken for
reaching toward the sword.
T herefore, in T enshinryu, when a sword is placed to the side, the alternative
method is used: set the fingertips of both hands upright in front of the

4, Sesshurei 折手礼 — Gyō 行 no Rei
Sesshurei 折手礼 is also commonly read
'Orite-rei 折手礼'.
From Ōgi-za 扇坐, first lower both hands to
the outsides of the thighs with the
fingertips pointing forward and place both
palms on the floor simultaneously. T hen,
keeping the fingertips pointing forward,
slide both hands forward until they are
beside the knees.
“折 Setsu or ori ” in Japanese means “ to
fold.” “手 Shu or te ” means “ hand.”

Sesshurei 折手礼 (O rite-rei)

T herefore, “ Sesshu ” or “ Orite ” literally means “ to fold the hand,” i.e., to
bend at the wrist.
Keep the hands open (hirade 開手), folding the thumbs slightly inward so
the thumbnails are not visible.
Since the hands are lowered laterally, this bow is not used when a sword is
placed (okigatana 置刀) on either side.
In the women's form of Sesshurei 折手礼, place both palms so that the
fingertips point to the rear.

5, Takushu-rei 拓手礼 — Gyō 行 no Rei
From Sesshurei 折手礼, advance the hands farther forward to in front of the
knees, place both palms on the floor, and incline the back until the chin
comes roughly over the hands. T he hands form a slightly inverted V shape.
Because the posture adds weight into the palms (tenohira 手の平) as if
taking a rubbing (taku 拓)*1, this bow is called Takushurei 拓手礼.
When returning the hands, unlike when extending them, bring the palms
straight back to their original position.
If a sword is set to the side, do not lower the hands laterally (which could be
mistaken as reaching for the sword); instead, slide them directly forward
from the thighs to in front of the knees.

*1 Here, taku means taking an impression by rubbing, as in a stone inscription rubbing
(takuhon 拓本) or a fish rubbing (gyotaku 魚拓).

6, Sōshu-rei — Shin 信 (真) no Rei
Sōshu-rei 雙手礼 (‘ double-hand bow'; also written Sōshu-rei 双手礼 with
the simplified character) advances the hands farther forward than
Takushu-rei 拓手礼 and is performed with a deeper forward inclination.
P osition the hands so that the line where the fingertips rested in Takushu-rei
拓手礼 now aligns roughly with the wrists in Sōshu-rei 雙手礼.
Use as a guide that, when the head is lowered, the mouth is directly above
the center of the triangle formed by the hands.
T he spacing between the hands is approximately the same as when the
hands hang at the groin line—about 4–5 cm apart.

Sōshu-rei 雙手礼



Set the hands down beginning with the hand on the side where the sword is
placed; this indicates that you are not releasing the blade from the koiguchi
(loosening the sword in the scabbard).
After the bow, when raising the face and returning the hands, do so in the
reverse order.
If the sword is placed behind you, or if you do not have the long sword at
hand, begin with the left hand to show that you are not releasing the blade
from the koiguchi of the wakizashi. When raising the face and returning the
hands, again reverse the order.
Regardless of where the sword is placed—or whether there is a sword at
all—there is also a method in which both hands are set down simultaneously;
in that case, return both hands simultaneously when raising the face.
If you are unsure which hand should go first, it is acceptable to use the
simultaneous method.

7. Gasshu-rei 合手礼 — Shin 真 (信) no Rei

Gasshu-rei 合手礼

Gasshu-rei 合手礼 corresponds to what is generally called heifuku 平伏
(prostration). It is the deepest and most respectful of the seated bows
normally performed.
P lace both elbows, forearms, and palms on the floor. Lower the upper body
until the chest touches or nearly touches the knees, with the back parallel to
the floor or inclined even more deeply.



Compared with sōshu-rei 雙手礼, the hands are placed farther forward and
the upper body is inclined more deeply. Move both hands forward from their
positions in sōshu-rei 雙手礼 by approximately half the length of a hand,
measured from the wrist to the fingertips.
As a guide, lower the head until the tip of the nose is directly above the
center of the triangle formed by the hands.
Keep the hands approximately 4–5 cm apart, roughly the same distance as when
they rest along the groin creases in ōgiza 扇坐.
Follow the same procedure as in sōshu-rei for placing the hands on the floor and
returning them to their original positions.

8. Gasshō-rei 合掌礼 — Shin 真 (信) no Rei



Gasshō-rei 合掌礼 is a seated bow used when paying respect to a person of
high rank. In T enshinryu, it is a special form of seated bow said to have
been used when visiting a daimyō or another high-ranking person as an envoy.
First, move both hands outward, then bring them together in front of you
and clap the palms together.
Next, interlace the fingers of both hands, with the fingers of the left hand
uppermost.
Keeping the fingers interlaced, lower the palms to the floor. P lace the palms,
forearms, and elbows on the floor, and bend the upper body forward.
Incline the back beyond a position parallel to the floor.
P osition the hands so that the two forearms form a single straight line, like
the Japanese character ‘一’ (ichimonji 一文字).
In general usage, however, gasshō-rei refers to a bow made with the palms
joined together in front of the chest when paying reverence to deities or
buddhas.

Sōkō-rei 爪甲礼 is a seated bow (zarei 坐礼) used primarily in koza 胡坐,
the cross-legged sitting posture. Although this bow was also widely
performed from ōgiza 扇坐 during the Edo period, T enshinryu does not
normally use it in that posture. T his is because, in the context of Edo-period
etiquette, closing the hands into fists could arouse suspicion that something
was being concealed in them.
In koza, the Sō no Rei 草の礼 takes the same forms as in ōgiza: meotoshi 目
落 and shurei 首礼. The Shin no Rei 真の礼 is performed with the palms
placed on the floor. Sōkō-rei 爪甲礼 is the Gyō no Rei 行の礼 performed
from koza.

10. Tekō-rei 手甲礼
Tekō-rei 手甲礼 is a traditional form
of etiquette in which one shows the
back of the hand to the other person.
In a broad sense, shiken-rei 指建礼
performed from ōgiza 扇坐 with the
fingertips placed on the floor in front
of the knees is also classified as
tekō-rei 手甲礼.
Assume an orishiki 折敷 posture with
the left knee on the ground and the
right knee raised. O pen the left hand
and extend it forward, placing the
fingertips on the ground in front of
the left knee.
Note that, depending on the situation,
one may instead take the opposite
orishiki 折敷 posture, with the right
knee placed on the ground in front of
the body and the left knee raised out
to the side. In this case, open the right
hand and place its fingertips on the
ground in front of the right knee to
perform the bow.
