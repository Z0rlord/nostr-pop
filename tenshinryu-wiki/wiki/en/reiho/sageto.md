# Sagetō 提刀 — Carrying the Sword in Hand

Carrying the long sword in one hand is sagetō (also written 下げ刀). Three forms, by situation (Kuwami 2026, "On Sagetō").

## 1. Ordinary sagetō

Cutting edge **up**, hilt forward, tip back, held by the scabbard (saya 鞘).

- **In training and demonstration:** right hand near the koiguchi 鯉口, thumb restraining the tsuba, against **sayabashiru 鞘走る** (the blade slipping out). The thumb sits on the **inner side, never directly over the edge**, so it is out of the blade's path.
- **In samurai daily life:** the scabbard was held beyond the kurikata 栗形, toward the kojiri 小尻. Holding that far down shows the blade cannot readily be released ([[concepts/koiguchi|koiguchi o kiru]]). The kurikata itself was avoided to keep the sageo free of hand oils.
- Facing a junior, or staying on guard, the sword may be carried in the **left hand**.

## 2. Ceremonial sagetō

Before a deity or a lord: cutting edge **down**, **tip forward, hilt back**, right hand near the koiguchi, the tsuba restrained by the **little-finger side of the palm**. With the hilt back there is no quick draw, the edge is not directed at the person, and the tip is lowered: a respectful posture of no hostile intent. Where other schools hold the scabbard beyond the kurikata, Tenshinryu restrains the guard as a precaution against someone gripping the hilt from behind. Here restraint is the rule, since it cannot serve a koiguchi release anyway.

## 3. Sagetō for a tachi 太刀

For a deeply curved tachi in a ceremonial setting: as the ceremonial form, but with the **cutting edge up**. A downward edge on a deep curve points the tip forward, and threat ranks tip first, edge second; reversing the edge avoids pointing the tip at the other person. It is also said to protect the scabbard from splitting and the edge from chipping if the sword is kicked while carried.
