# Ritsurei 立礼 and Zarei 坐礼 — Standing and Seated Bows

Tenshinryu preserves a teaching attributed to Yagyū Munenori: **"Within the Nine Rites lies the secret principle" (九礼に極意あり, Kurei ni gokui ari)**, matching the Kuhon-rei 九品礼 of the Ogasawara-ryu 小笠原流 etiquette tradition. Reihō is the core of hyoho: it honors the other party, keeps harmony, prevents conflict, cultivates mind and body, and underlies the techniques themselves. Never treat it lightly (Kuwami 2026, "On Reihō").

## Shin, Gyō, Sō — the three grades

Every bow, standing or seated, falls into one of three grades by depth:

| Grade | Kanji | Use | Depth and timing |
|-------|-------|-----|------------------|
| Shin | 信 (真) | high rank; prostration (heifuku 平伏) or worship (hai 拝) | Head deep, like a hanging wisteria cluster; hold 5 counts or about 3 breaths. Before a lord, keep the head down until permitted, and do not snap up when permitted |
| Gyō | 行 | greeting a superior (jōshi 上士) | Head lowered like a ripened rice ear; hold 3 counts or about 2 breaths |
| Sō | 草 | peers and subordinates; also replying to a superior | Slight lower of the head; 2 counts or about 1 breath |

Any posture may also be held to wait or listen.

## Gaze

Avert the gaze from the other party; toward a superior, lower the eyes to their chest. Averting the eyes is also a sign of trust: a deep bow with a raised chin and glaring eyes is discourteous. In prostration, a warrior discreetly uses an **upward glance (uwamezukai)** to read the other party and the room without being noticed, the mindset of jōzai-senjō 常在戦場 ("always on the battlefield"). First acquire correct form; only then add eye control (mekubari 目配り), or the bow itself falls apart.

## Ritsurei 立礼 — standing bows (chiefly outdoors)

Bend **from the hips, not the neck**: dropping from the head rounds the back and leaves you unready. Use uwamezukai only in the Shin bow; in Gyō and Sō it would be noticed, and the lowered gaze already keeps the room in view.

1. **Shin 信(真):** incline the back until the midpoints of the hands reach the centers of the knees. Deeper still, nearly horizontal, is **hairei 拝礼**, for deities, buddhas, or the highest ranks of warrior or court aristocracy; for still greater ceremony one kneels into **yagura-rei 矢倉礼**.
2. **Gyō 行:** incline until both hands reach about mid-thigh.
3. **Sō 草:** the eshaku 会釈 (slight bow): body light, hands resting on the thighs just below the groin line, or both hands in [[concepts/hand-shapes|Ika-no-kuchi]] lowered at the sides. In Tenshinryu also called **yū 揖**.

## Zarei 坐礼 — seated bows (indoors)

Ten forms, learned so they can be applied by situation:

1. **Me-otoshi 目落 / mokurei 目礼 (Sō):** the lightest eshaku. The eyeballs do not move; the upper body inclines slightly and the line of sight lowers. Do not tilt the neck alone. Used to return a bow or reply.
2. **Shurei 首礼 (Sō):** a shade deeper than me-otoshi, still an eshaku; incline the upper body so the head tilts naturally.
3. **Shikenrei 指建礼 (Gyō):** from Ōgiza, fingertips of both open hands upright on the floor; the training-hall and demonstration workhorse. Common form places fingertips beside the knees; **when a sword is set to the side, place them in front of the knees** so the reach is not mistaken for moving toward the sword.
4. **Sesshurei 折手礼 (also Orite-rei; Gyō):** hands lower to the outsides of the thighs, palms down simultaneously, then slide forward beside the knees, fingertips forward. Not used with a sword placed at the side. In the women's form the palms point to the rear.
5. **Takushu-rei 拓手礼 (Gyō):** from sesshurei, palms advance in front of the knees in a shallow inverted V; the back inclines until the chin is roughly over the hands, weighting the palms like taking a rubbing (taku 拓). Return the palms straight back. With a side sword, slide directly forward, not laterally.
6. **Sōshu-rei 雙手礼 (Shin):** hands advance beyond takushu-rei; where the fingertips were now sits the wrist line. Mouth above the center of the hands' triangle, hands 4-5 cm apart. **Set down first the hand on the side where the sword is placed**, showing the blade is not being loosened; return in reverse. If the sword is behind you or only the wakizashi is at hand, start with the left. A simultaneous both-hands method exists for any case, and is acceptable when unsure.
7. **Gasshu-rei 合手礼 (Shin):** the deepest ordinary seated bow, general heifuku. Elbows, forearms and palms on the floor, chest to or near the knees, back parallel to the floor or deeper. Hands half a hand-length beyond sōshu-rei; nose above the triangle's center; same hand order as sōshu-rei.
8. **Gasshō-rei 合掌礼 (Shin):** the special envoy's bow before a daimyō or high personage. Hands move out, clap together, interlace fingers with the left hand uppermost; palms, forearms and elbows to the floor, forearms forming one straight line (ichimonji 一文字), back inclined beyond parallel. (In general usage, gasshō means the palms-together bow before deities.)
9. **Sōkō-rei 爪甲礼 (Gyō, from Koza):** open both hands to show them empty, place the backs of the hands before the knees, then form [[concepts/hand-shapes|Ika-no-kuchi]] fists, left hand outside the left knee, right outside the right, and bow. Edo-period practice also did this from ōgiza, but Tenshinryu does not: closed fists could suggest something concealed. From Koza, the Sō bows are me-otoshi and shurei as in ōgiza, and the Shin bow places the palms on the floor.
10. **Tekō-rei 手甲礼:** showing the back of the hand. From [[reiho/zaho-seated-postures|Orishiki]] with the left knee down and right knee raised, extend the open left hand and set its fingertips before the left knee. The mirrored posture (right knee down) takes the right hand. In a broad sense, the front-of-knees shikenrei also belongs to tekō-rei.
