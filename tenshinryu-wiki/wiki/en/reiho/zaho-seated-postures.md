# Zahō 座法 — Seated Postures

Zahō is the method of sitting. Tenshinryu deliberately distinguishes two kanji: **座** for the act or method of sitting, **坐** for techniques performed from a seated position (as in 坐法納刀, seated nōtō). This page catalogs the postures; for rising and sitting mechanics see [[reiho/kikyo-chakuza]] (Kuwami 2026, "Zahō").

## Ōgiza 扇坐 — the basic posture

The most-used sitting posture in Tenshinryu: kneeling, **knees opened two to three fist-widths** (fists laid horizontal). Named for the knees spreading like an opened folding fan (ōgi 扇); also called **otokoza / otokozuwari 男坐**, the "man's sitting posture".

- As a rule the **tops of the feet are not overlapped**, so you can move freely in an emergency. For long sitting, one foot may be placed over the other to prevent numbness.
- Exception: when positioned for jōi-uchi (上意討ち), the foot on the side you will step out with is deliberately placed on top.
- Hands in [[concepts/hand-shapes|Hirade]] rest slightly inward along the groin creases, fingers down, elbows in: respectful without looking martial, and the wakizashi stays reachable.
- On formal occasions the hands may be placed into the hakama's side openings (waki-aki 脇開き): a ceremonial sign that one does not intend to draw. Doing it casually, for cold hands or for style, is a shameful breach; Edo-period etiquette manuals forbid it outside ceremony.

**Ōgiza vs seiza:** Tenshinryu, like Edo-period samurai, does not use seiza 正坐 (knees together). Seiza was the posture of lower status and of women; the word itself spread only after Meiji. Earlier, knees-together sitting was called tanza 端座 or kashikomaru 畏まる.

## Koza 胡坐 (Darumaza 達磨坐, Anza 安坐)

Cross-legged sitting, used especially in **Koshiki-no-Kata / Koden-no-Kata 古式の勢法** (pre-Edo, armored battlefield methods). Standard before the Edo period and still used formally during it; reading it as the common "agura" is not wrong. Also called Darumaza after Bodhidharma's seated meditation, and Anza 安坐 for its relaxed ease.

- Hands closed in [[concepts/hand-shapes|Oyayubi-Kakushi]], palms up, backs down: left hand on left knee, right hand at the base of the right thigh. Tucked thumbs signal cutting neither the koiguchi nor gripping the tsuka.
- **Kami-no-ashi 上の足:** when the kamiza (上座, seat of honor) or a superior is on the Yō (left) or Mei (front) side, the **left foot is placed forward**. This avoids pointing toes or soles at the honored person and voluntarily restricts one's own movement. The opposite side is shimo-no-ashi 下の足.
- In armor (suneate 脛当て shin guards), the front foot relaxes slightly forward; this may also be done unarmored.

## Hanza 半坐 / Fuza 趺坐

Half Ōgiza, half Koza: one leg tucked under with the heel under the body, the other bent outward like Koza. Hands as in Koza; leg order follows Koza conventions. The slightly raised hips allow **quicker movement than Koza**. In Tenshinryu, Fuza specifically means Hanza (the character 趺 points to the instep placed beneath the buttocks).

## Kiza 跪坐 / Tsukubai 蹲

From Ōgiza: knees closed together, **toes raised**, buttocks on heels. Used when transitioning into movement, or waiting poised. In Tenshinryu, Tsukubai refers to this same Kiza-like posture (elsewhere "tsukubai" usually means squatting, or the tea-garden water basin).

## Yagura 矢倉 / 櫓

Kiza with the **knees opened outward**: both knees grounded, toes raised. Used in Koshiki forms, especially waiting outdoors or on the battlefield. Named for a watchtower/arrow-storehouse, from the leg shape.

## Orishiki 折敷

One knee forward on the floor, the other raised to the side: also called **tatehiza 立膝**, **katahiza-dachi 片膝立ち**, or warihiza 割膝. Used in Koshiki and in seated nōtō (Zahō Nōtō 坐法納刀) as a posture of readiness or restraint.

## Sonkyo 蹲踞

A squat: hips lowered, heels raised, buttocks over the heels, knees open. Used in kumidachi 組太刀, in etiquette with long weapons (nagamono 長物), and in Koshiki forms when waiting outdoors or on the battlefield. (A different knees-together half-crouch also exists in general usage as the waiting posture of attendants such as chūgen 中間.)
