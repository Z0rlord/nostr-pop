However, in today's training environment, according to Ishii Sensei of a former

"Train step by step" is something said not only in Tenshinryu but in every martial
art and traditional art.
In general, however, many martial arts teach students to complete the Kaisho or
Shu stage fully before moving on to the next stage.
In contrast, Tenshinryu teaches students to move on to training of the next stage,
even if it is premature.
In other words, even if the Kaisho or Shu stage is still insufficient, the next stages—
Gyōsho or Ha, and Sōsho or Ri—are taught.
This can be said to derive from the educational system of the Shirin-dan, whose
aim was not to produce masters in the distant future, but to train people who could
fulfil their duties as samurai.
However, explaining all of this in this book would make the volume of information
enormous, so this book limits its explanations to the Kaisho and Shu stage.

士林団

Also, unlike the samurai of the Shirin-dan (
) of old, the reality is that it is
not easy for people today to secure enough time for training.
Therefore, with this book as an aid, first aim to acquire the Kaisho and Shu stage
firmly.
This also has the effect of avoiding detours and preventing needless injuries.
About Breaking Down the Movements
Kaisho and Shu refer to the method and stage of training in which the movement
is divided into parts, in order to acquire the technique firmly and to make learning
easier as the first stage of the technique.
For some techniques, a representative way of dividing them has been clearly
handed down, but basically a technique can be divided in countless ways, and the

division can change without limit according to the stage and purpose of the
training.
Since it is impossible to introduce all of them here, only one pattern that can be
called representative is introduced.
If you train firmly in what is described here, you will basically be able to acquire
other divisions easily as well.
About Jo, Ha and Kyū

序

Basically, training is first done at Jo ( ) (slow). Then, as you become familiar with
the movement, you train at Ha ( ) (medium) and Kyū ( ) (fast).

破

急
However, some movements—such as taking Seigan ( 青眼 , the middle guard),
Chiburui ( 血振い , shaking the blood off the blade) and Nōtō ( 納刀 , sheathing)—
are basically done at Kyū, except at the beginner stage when the movements are still
uncertain.

This is because repeating these particular movements at Jo does not lead to im‐
provement. When done slowly, some movements put a local load on the muscles,
or the sequence breaks down and the movement falls apart. Therefore, in such
cases, they are done at Kyū unless there is a very good reason not to.
Apart from such exceptions, the speed of Jo remains important regardless of your
level.
In Tenshinryu, even at an early stage, you learn Ha and Kyū as soon as you become
accustomed to the technique.
This is because there are things that cannot be acquired through Jo alone, and
above all, training at practical speed is indispensable for refining a technique to a
practical level.
At the same time, however advanced you may be, if you forget Jo in your training,
your progress will reach its limit.
Goals of Training
For every technique introduced in this book, the following three items are first set
as goals.
1. Remember the name
Tenshinryu has a great many techniques, and it is difficult to memorize all of them
perfectly.
However, for smooth training and a deeper understanding of the Style and its tech‐
niques, it is desirable to memorize as much as possible.
Try, as far as possible, to connect the name of each technique with its meaning and
its form.
2. Remember the characteristics (Maai, Situation)
It goes without saying that remembering the sequence, form and movements de‐
scribed in the next item is important.
However, no matter how well you remember them, if you do not correctly under‐
stand and remember the premises—such as Maai (
, distance) and the Situation
—your movements will end up being wrong.

間合

Therefore, make sure you firmly understand and remember the preconditions of
each technique.
3. Remember the sequence, form and movements
Basically, this is the very purpose of training, yet surprisingly many people remem‐
ber it carelessly or remember it wrongly.
With the determination to read between the lines—as the Japanese saying goes,
"the light of one's eyes piercing to the back of the paper"—read the descriptions
correctly, understand them, and learn them with your body.

Points of Caution
— Always hold the tsuba down with the thumb

鞘走る

To prevent sayabashiri (
, the sword slipping out of the scabbard uninten‐
tionally), you must always hold the tsuba down with your thumb. When doing so,
take care not to place the thumb directly above the edge.
If the sword should slip out of the scabbard, there is a danger of cutting the pad of
the thumb.
— Do not lean forward
In Tenshinryu, leaning forward is important in many techniques, but in ordinary
Tsuka-uchi the upper body is kept almost vertical.
— Stand on tiptoe
When striking the Taisha with the tsuka, keep the front foot on tiptoe with the
heel well raised (Uki-ashi), and take care to keep the knee as high as possible.
— About the rear knee and shin
When striking the Taisha with the tsuka, always advance the rear knee until it is
close enough to touch the front foot. Take care not to leave it behind.
Also, the shin of the rear leg should point almost sideways. This is necessary to take
Hanmi.

— About Ken-Ki-Tai Ichigan
The timing differs between Kaisho (Shu) and Sōsho (Ri). In Kaisho (Shu), strike at
the moment the rear foot comes forward; in Sōsho (Ri), strike at the moment the
front foot steps in.
— Step back far
In reality, it is very difficult to step straight back without changing feet and take a
distance of six shaku (180 cm) or more.
However, by training to step back as far as possible, you acquire the footwork re‐
quired in Tenshinryu and strengthen your legs and hips.
Basically, the standard is that your shins must not be cut if the Taisha, in despera‐
tion, draws and sweeps with the wakizashi or daitō on the spot.
(Note that in this case, the Taisha is not assumed to move forward to attack.)
The reason for drawing back the front foot into Shumoku-ashi is that, by making
the centre of gravity a single point, you can turn in any direction and move a great
distance. Another reason is to guard against the most dangerous attack: a cut to the
shins.
— Do not step straight back
If you step straight back, it is difficult to step back far, so not only can you not take
sufficient distance, you also cannot get off the Taisha's line of attack.
Take care to step back slightly diagonally to the left.
At this time, take care to keep facing the Taisha.
— About Shumoku-ashi
Facing the Taisha, the kojiri should be just outside your right knee.

船漕ぎ

Also, be ready to perform Funakogi (
, "rowing a boat")—stepping the right
foot forward immediately and striking with the kojiri—if the Taisha attacks in
desperation.
Immediately take the Tsukagashira down with the left hand in Tatsu no Kuchi, and
operate the sword with the right hand to thrust or strike at the Taisha's face, etc.

About the Three Kinds of "Nukiuchi"
In Tenshinryu, "nukiuchi" is broadly divided into three kinds by the use of differ‐
ent kanji.

抜刀 (nukiuchi) — This simply means drawing the sword. Sometimes, to show
the difference between other styles and Tenshinryu, it refers to drawing the sword
in a private quarrel or needlessly.

抜討 (nukiuchi) — This means Jōi-uchi (上意討ち: striking down a criminal or
the like on orders from the domain, the shogunate or a superior samurai).

抜打 (nukiuchi) — Katsujinken, which controls a conflict without cutting, using
the mune or shinogi instead of the edge. In particular, the mune (and its use) is
called Kattō ( 活刀 ) or Watō ( 和刀 ) in Tenshinryu, and is taught as something
special.

In the cultural sphere of kanji, meaning is given to words. This is because words
have been thought to hold great power.
This culture is strongly reflected in Tenshinryu. Even in the term "nukiuchi", the
mission and thought of the Shirin-dan Kōgan are embodied by using different
kanji in this way.

The shōtō in particular is also called Mamori-gatana ( 守り刀 , protective sword).

長脇差

In Tenshinryu, "wakizashi" is written

It is called wakizashi in the sense of an auxiliary weapon worn beside (waki) the
honzashi (
, the daitō, long sword), which is the main weapon. In Tenshinryu,
however, with the meaning that there is no high or low among weapons, the for‐
mal names are defined as shōtō and kodachi, meaning simply a small, shorter
sword.

本差

Taking the kodachi, which is particularly useful in actual combat, as the basis, the
group of seated techniques using the shōtō is defined as kodachi.
Some techniques are special, using the kodachi (naga-wakizashi) in particular, or a
shorter denchū-zashi (
, sword worn inside the palace) or a tantō (
,
dagger). For the other techniques, however, it is not the case that they can only be
practiced or used with a kodachi; even in the groups of techniques that bear the
name kodachi, there is no problem using a wakizashi of the generally used standard
length (about one shaku four sun, approx. 42 cm).

One of the characteristics of Tenshinryu is that it transmits a very wide variety of

Points of Caution
— When placing the hands on the sword
When placing the hands on the sword, take care not to open the armpits. Keep
both elbows against the left and right ribs and do not let them separate.
As a test, try doing it with a tenugui (hand towel) or the like held under both
armpits (upper arms). If the tenugui does not fall before both hands are on the
sword, there is no problem.
— About the draw
When drawing, take care that the tsuba passes along your Jinchū-ro (
centre line of the body) and does not stray to the left or right.

人中路, the

— About Saya-biki
Because the wakizashi is short and easy to draw, Saya-biki tends to be insufficient.
Saya-biki is not simply for drawing the sword; it is an essential movement for tak‐
ing the correct position of the shoulders and elbows and for balancing the whole
body. Do not think of it merely as a movement for drawing—pull the left fist and
the scabbard back sufficiently.
Also take care that the te no uchi of the left hand holds the koiguchi correctly.
— When taking the tsuka with the left hand
When taking the tsuka with the left hand, take care not to open the palm too much,
and do not let the left hand and left elbow open outward and trace a curve.
The left hand and left elbow should trace a straight line to take the tsuka.

— Open both knees evenly
You open both knees when cutting down; take care to open them evenly to the left
and right.
Do not open only one knee.
— Do not open the knees too far
You are told to open the knees as wide as you can, but seen from the front, if the
angle between the thighs exceeds 80–90 degrees,
they are opened too far.
The hips become too low and the hip joints are excessively open, so you cannot
move on to the next movement immediately.
— About the sword and the right arm
When you have cut down, as a guide, the sword and the right arm should form a
straight line. Take care that the wrist is neither under-turned nor over-turned.
— About Orishiki (Katahiza-dachi)
In Orishiki (Katahiza-dachi), take care not to lean the upper body forward. Also
take care not to tilt the body left or right; keep the upper body upright, with verti‐
cal as the guide.
The knees are opened to roughly a right angle.
The front knee opens slightly outward from straight ahead, and the rear knee is
slightly closed rather than straight out to the side.
Also, raise the rear knee as high as possible, to chest height.
— Zanshin in Orishiki
The kissaki points at the Nakazumi, aimed so that if the Taisha grabs at you in des‐
peration, you can step in with the right foot and make a one-handed thrust with
the right hand as it is.

— About Nōtō
After sheathing Kissaki Sanzun, continue sheathing while moving the scabbard
slightly faster than the sword.
— When finishing seated
After step 17 of the Movement Sequence, you may also finish by sitting down
(Chakuza) directly from Kiza.
In that case, from Kiza, put the insteps of both feet on the floor and come into Ogizahō.
Then, as when finishing standing, sheathe the sword completely, place both hands
in Hirate at the base of the thighs, then lower both hands to the inner thighs, and
finish by sitting in Seiza.

Points of Caution
— When standing the sword up
When standing the sword at the left side, raise it so that it is vertical when seen
from the front.
Also, strictly keep the tsuba at chin height. Because of the structure of the skeleton,
the best position for the height of the tsuba is more or less fixed.
— Do not twist the chest
From standing the sword up until cutting down, do not twist the chest (sternum)
to the left; keep it facing forward.
— Extend the elbow when drawing
Basically, extend the body as much as possible.
Extend the right elbow as far as you can.
— About the left hand
After drawing, when taking the tsuka with the left hand, move the left fist and left
elbow in a straight line. In many cases they open outward (to the left) and trace a
curve.
— About standing on tiptoe
A mistake beginners often make is rising onto the toes while drawing.
Take care to rise onto the toes together with the cut down.

— About arching the body
When you rise onto the toes, arch the whole body so that the hips come forward of
the feet. Take care not simply to bend the hips backward, but to arch the whole
body like a bow.
— About the kissaki
If standing on tiptoe or arching the body is insufficient, you may hit the floor with
the kissaki, so take sufficient care.
— About cutting down
Cut down keeping the elbow extended as it was when you drew upward. When
swinging something, people instinctively bend the elbow. As a result, from the po‐
sition where the fist was raised at the draw, the fist and sword are pulled further
back before cutting down, and the cut is delayed.
Therefore, it is essential to keep the elbow extended and swing the fist and sword
straight down from the top of the draw.
— About Hizagoshi
The cut down is Hizagoshi. At this time, the right upper arm, right forearm and
sword form roughly a straight line.
— About the knees
When swinging the sword down you rise onto the toes, but bend the knees
slightly.
If you rise onto the toes with the knees fully extended, you become unstable and
the essential arching of the hips and back becomes insufficient.
— About Chiburui and Nōtō
In Chiburui, perform it quickly from the raising movement just before shaking off
the blood.
Raise the fist to the right; when it reaches about chest height, quickly raise it fur‐
ther to about shoulder height and quickly bring it down to perform Chiburui.

Then, while quickly turning the sword over, quickly send the scabbard forward
with the left hand and quickly sheathe up to Kissaki Sanzun.

The name Tsurube-otoshi (

The wooden bucket of a tsurube well is quite heavy, so it falls to the bottom of the
well at tremendous speed under its own weight. From this, "tsurube-otoshi" came
to be widely used as a term meaning to fall quickly or with force.
The best-known example is probably the proverb "Aki no yūhi wa tsurube-otoshi"

sets. There are also waterfalls in various parts of Japan named "Tsurube-otoshi no
Taki," and in western Japan a yōkai (supernatural creature) that drops down from
above is called Tsurube-otoshi (or Tsurube-oroshi).
It is a technique for training, and its main purpose is to forge the body.

Points of Caution
— Do not push the fist forward when drawing
Do not let the fist come forward when drawing. Draw as if thrusting the right fist
straight up.
— Keep the right arm against the face
Do not let the right upper arm separate from the right side of the head; raise the
right arm almost perpendicular to the upper body.
— Extend the right arm
Extend the right elbow and take care not to bend it.
— Do not raise the kissaki
Cut and stop the Taisha's draw with the image of catching fingers in a sliding door.
As the name suggests, like a fishing line held at a slant, take care not to flick the
kissaki up, and keep the blade at a slant.
However, do not stop the kissaki deliberately; take care that it comes to a stop
naturally.

Afterword

I intended to publish as simple a book as possible, but it became more voluminous
than I had imagined.
I have written only the minimum content, yet the fact that it still amounts to this
much information is, I think, a sign of how important the beginner's stage is.
Anyone who has trained to some extent should already have received explanations
and been taught almost all of this content. In reality, however, human beings let
much of it slip away.
According to one study, the amount of information that can be remembered cor‐
rectly from an oral explanation is about 47% on average.
In writing it is 58%, and with video about 67%. Since actual martial arts instruction
includes not only oral explanation but also demonstration, it may be said to be close
to video—yet even so, by this calculation 33% is lost (conditions and individual dif‐
ferences will be considerable, but as a rough guide).
I hope this book will lead to such a review, help you progress further, and serve as a
good guide for those who wish to study Tenshinryu seriously from now on.
These ten techniques come with supplementary knowledge and various applica‐
tions and variations, but if you first thoroughly acquire the content of this book,
mastering them will become easier as you grow more proficient.
Likewise, because the tactical, technical and motor principles are shared, it will
make mastering every technique easier.
One also sees the argument that basics are always the same repetition and therefore
meaningless.
Certainly, if one is repeating basics at the same level in the first year, the fifth, the
tenth and the twentieth, it is merely a waste of time.
But that is an example of receiving instruction of a very regrettable level. If one has
trained correctly, the quality will be completely different even with the same basic
content.

And however excellent the instruction, whether it is brought to life or wasted de‐
pends on the practitioner.
If you keep to the teachings, the technique will surely answer your efforts.
Do not make light of the basics; train with dedication.
